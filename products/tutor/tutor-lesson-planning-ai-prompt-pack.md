# Tutor Lesson Planning AI Prompt Pack

### Plan lessons, quizzes and study guides 5x faster (50+ prompts)

**Format: PDF + Notion**

Tutors and teachers do not fail because they lack ideas — they fail because the ideas are trapped in a head that also has to mark homework, chase absent students, write parent emails, prep a quiz, and remember what the student covered three weeks ago. This pack turns the work you already know how to do into copy-paste prompts you can fill in, adapt and reuse: 50+ prompts for lesson plans, quizzes, worksheets, homework and progress reports, 10 lesson template structures scaffolded for any subject and level, and differentiation built in so that every prompt adapts to student level, learning style and curriculum. You keep the professional judgement; the AI does the first draft, the formatting, the tiering and the admin.

## How to use this pack

**1. Fill in the placeholders, then paste.** Every prompt in this pack uses `{{double_brace}}` placeholders. Before you paste, replace each one with real detail. If a placeholder is genuinely unknown, write `UNKNOWN` and add a line at the end of the prompt: *"Where I have written UNKNOWN, ask me up to 5 clarifying questions before you begin."* This is the single biggest difference between a useful AI lesson plan and a generic one — detail in, specificity out.

**2. Give the AI a role and a standard.** The prompts below are written as complete messages: they set the role, the audience, the constraints and the output format. Paste them whole rather than trimming them down. The "Output format" lines are what stop the AI from returning a wall of prose when you need a table, and what stop it from inventing a lesson with no timings.

**3. Work in the order that saves the most time.** A reliable working sequence for a normal teaching week:

| Step | Use this | Time saved |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Anchor the lesson | Template 1 (standard 50-minute lesson) + Learning Objectives category | 20–30 min |
| 2. Build the materials | Activities, Worksheets & Handouts categories | 30–45 min |
| 3. Check understanding | Quizzes & Assessment category + exit ticket prompt | 15–25 min |
| 4. Set the follow-up | Homework & Follow-up category | 5–10 min |
| 5. Communicate | Progress Reports & Parent Communication category | 15–20 min |
| 6. Improve next time | Lesson Reflection & Improvement category | 5 min |

**4. Lock your standards with a reusable brief.** Most quality problems come from the AI not knowing your curriculum, your exam board, your students' starting points or your house style. Create a one-page *Teaching Brief* (your own template or the starter in the Online & Distance Teaching category) and paste it at the top of any prompt that involves content or assessment. Include: curriculum/exam board, topics already covered, topics still to come, typical class dynamics, what "good" looks like in your studio, and your preferred formatting. Keep it in your Notion page and copy the same block every time.

**5. Review, don't publish.** AI-generated content will contain plausible errors: an equation that doesn't balance, a historical date that is off by a decade, a question that assesses something you never taught, a "differentiation" that simply makes the task harder rather than more accessible. Use the built-in checks: ask the AI to list every factual claim it made that you should verify, and ask it to flag where a student is likely to make a common misconception. Your professional signature stays on the lesson.

**6. Iterate in conversation, not from scratch.** When an output is nearly right, don't re-paste the original prompt — give feedback: *"Keep the structure and timings. Make the extension task more open-ended. The reading level is too high for {{level}} — rewrite the extract at a reading age of {{reading_age}}."* Iteration is faster than regeneration and preserves the good parts.

**7. Build your own bank.** Star the prompts that work for you and keep a personal "my favourites" list in Notion. Swap the subject, level and duration but keep the structure. The pack is designed to be used repeatedly, not once.

### A note on levels

This pack refers to UK-style levels (KS3, KS4, GCSE, A-level) because that is where most tutor businesses start, but every prompt works for any system. Where a level is written into a prompt, treat it as a variable:

| Level | Typical learner | What "differentiation" means here |
|---|---|---|
| KS3 (ages 11–14) | Building foundations, often below expected | Concrete examples, chunked instructions, lots of guided practice |
| KS4 / GCSE (14–16) | Working toward a grade | Exam command words, mark schemes, timed practice, targeted feedback |
| A-level (16–19) | Deep conceptual understanding | Second-order reasoning, contradictory evidence, independent research |
| Undergraduate | Academic study skills | Argument, source evaluation, essay structure, discipline-specific method |
| Adult beginner | Learning for work, confidence or pleasure | Real-world context, low anxiety, unsanctioned mistakes, pace control |
| Exam prep (any level) | Time pressure and a target grade | High-frequency practice, error analysis, timed conditions, mock feedback |

### A note on learning styles

You will see `{{learning_style}}` placeholders throughout. The pack deliberately uses it as a *preference and accessibility* variable (a learner who needs a visual model, a learner who needs step-by-step verbal explanation, a learner who needs hands-on practice, a learner who needs a printed structure to follow) rather than as a rigid "everyone is a visual learner" claim. If you are not sure, use `mixed — adapt by topic rather than by person` and ask for at least two representations of the key idea.

### The templates vs the prompts

The **10 lesson template structures** are skeletons — the shape of a lesson, with timings, required sections and decision points already laid out. The **50+ prompts** are the working parts you drop into any skeleton: objectives, activities, quizzes, worksheets, homework, differentiation, parent communication and reflection. In practice you use them together: pick a template, generate the content with the prompts, then edit by hand.

# 10 Lesson Template Structures — scaffolded for any subject and level

Each template below is a fill-in skeleton: the timings, required sections, decision points and scaffolding are already done. Pick the shape that matches your session, copy it into your notes, and use the prompts in later sections to fill each part. A template is not a lesson — it is the armature on which the prompts build one.

For every template, start by replacing the **Top line** variables, then use the placeholder prompts to generate each marked block. The "Decisions to make yourself" box at the end of each template tells you exactly where your professional judgement must take over — never delegate those to the AI.

## Template 1 — Standard 50-minute lesson

**Use when:** a routine single-topic lesson, any subject, any level. The backbone most tutors teach on; all other templates are variations of this shape.

**Top line:** Subject: `{{subject}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Topic: `{{topic}}` | Student/class: `{{student_or_class}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Do-now / starter | A 5-minute retrieval task that reactivates last lesson's key idea. No new content. | Activity: Starter prompt |
| 05–12 | Learning objectives + connect | State what success looks like today (2-3 objectives); link to what the student already knows. | Learning Objectives category |
| 12–30 | Teach + model | Present the new idea in one chunk. Model one worked example aloud, showing the thinking. | Lesson Plan Design + Activities |
| 30–40 | Guided practice | Student attempts 3-5 similar problems together with you; you scaffold, prompt, correct. | Activities: Guided practice |
| 40–46 | Independent practice | Student works alone on 2-3 problems; you observe and note where they stall. | Activities: Independent practice |
| 46–50 | Check for understanding | Exit task or 2 quick questions to confirm the objective landed; tell you the plan for next lesson. | Quizzes: Exit ticket |

**Decision points to make yourself:** the one key idea that carries the whole lesson; the precise success criteria; how much the student already knows (don't guess — ask or test); which misconception you expect and how you'll pre-empt it.

**Why this timing works:** it front-loads retrieval, keeps teaching to one tight chunk, and guarantees the student does more thinking than you do. If you are short on time, never cut the independent practice — cut the teach.

## Template 2 — 90-minute workshop

**Use when:** a longer session, a small group, a higher level, or a topic that needs sustained exploration. Feels different from two 45-minute lessons — it should build momentum rather than double the content.

**Top line:** Subject: `{{subject}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Workshop title: `{{workshop_title}}` | Duration: 90 min | Group size: `{{group_size}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–10 | Warm-up + framing | Quick retrieval; set the workshop question; agree the outcome ("by the end you'll be able to..."). | Activities: Starter |
| 10–30 | Input (one chunk) | 20 minutes of teaching max. If the topic genuinely needs more, do it in two 10-minute chunks with a task between. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 30–55 | First task | A structured task the whole group attempts; you circulate. Harder than the routine lesson's guided practice — the point is productive struggle. | Activities: Group task |
| 55–70 | Critique + refine | Students swap work or you bring up 2 anonymised attempts; the group improves them together. | Activities: Peer feedback |
| 70–83 | Extended application | A fresh, slightly harder problem that forces transfer; students work mostly alone. | Activities: Extension task |
| 83–90 | Synthesis + close | Whole group: what did we learn, what would you do differently, what's our one question for next time. | Reflection prompts |

**Decision points to make yourself:** the workshop question (make it genuinely debatable or genuinely hard); the "extended application" task — it must be new, not a repeat of the first task; how you will handle the spread of pace across the group.

**Why 90 minutes works:** you have room for two practice cycles and a genuine critique moment. The workshop's value is in the application and the refinement, not in a longer lecture.

## Template 3 — 1-to-1 tutoring session

**Use when:** a single student, the most common tutoring shape. The structure is the same as a lesson, but the emphasis shifts: the session is a conversation built around the student's specific gaps, not a class.

**Top line:** Student: `{{student_name}}` | Subject: `{{subject}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Today's goal: `{{goal}}` | Last session covered: `{{last_session}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Connect + recall | Check homework, ask what stuck last time. This is diagnostic: what they say shapes the session. | Homework & Follow-up |
| 05–12 | Precise target | Agree the one thing we will get better at today. Write it down. In 1-to-1 this agreement is what makes the session feel bespoke. | Learning Objectives |
| 12–30 | Teach + go deep | Teach the single idea, but push further than a class would: ask "why", probe the edge cases, let them explain it back. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 30–45 | Application | The student works through problems while you watch their process, not just their answer. Pause at the exact moment they hesitate. | Activities |
| 45–55 | Consolidation | Student produces a 3-line summary of the rule in their own words; then a problem they solve alone to prove it. | Quizzes |
| 55–60 | Set next | Agree homework, name the next goal, and decide what to bring next time. Record it for the parent report. | Progress Reports |

**Decision points to make yourself:** the *actual* gap (a student's stated problem is often not the real one — ask "show me how you did it"); how hard to push the application (1-to-1 is where you can afford to let them struggle productively); what the student should teach you by the end (the ultimate check).

**Why this template is different:** in a class you aim at the middle; in 1-to-1 you aim at the edge of the student's current ability. The session should feel like it was built for them because it was — cut the padding, go where they are.

## Template 4 — Exam revision lesson

**Use when:** a student is preparing for a specific exam, at any level (GCSE, A-level, undergrad, professional). Revision is a skill in itself — the lesson should teach the strategy as much as the content.

**Top line:** Exam: `{{exam}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Paper/unit: `{{paper_or_unit}}` | Topic: `{{topic}}` | Time until exam: `{{weeks_left}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–08 | Diagnose the target | Look at the syllabus or a past paper: what is the highest-yield topic you have not yet mastered? Pick ONE. | Lesson Plan Design + Quizzes |
| 08–18 | Technique mini-review | Re-read the core notes for that topic in 10 minutes — but the point is recall, so close the notes and write the key points from memory. | Activities: Retrieval |
| 18–38 | Timed exam question | One real past-paper question, timed to exam conditions. State the marks available and the time you have. | Quizzes: Exam-style |
| 38–50 | Mark + analyse | Mark it against the mark scheme together. Separate "wrong answer" from "weak technique" (didn't read the command word; didn't show working; ran out of time). | Lesson Reflection |
| 50–60 | Fix + record | Fix one specific weakness — a technique, not just the content. Record the error in the student's error log; set a targeted homework. | Homework & Follow-up |

**Decision points to make yourself:** which single topic to target (the highest-yield *unmastered* topic, not the favourite); whether the problem is knowledge or technique — the mark scheme tells you; how to handle the last two weeks before the exam (strategy shifts from learning to consolidating and marking).

**Why this template is different:** a revision lesson is *not* a teaching lesson with a timer. The output is an error log and a refined technique, not new understanding. If a student needs teaching, you are not in revision mode yet.

## Template 5 — Practical / hands-on lab

**Use when:** science practicals, computing labs, design, art, music, languages (speaking work), any session where the learning happens by doing. The risk is that the doing crowds out the learning — the template keeps the thinking explicit.

**Top line:** Subject: `{{subject}}` | Practical/task: `{{practical_task}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Safety/kit notes: `{{safety_kit}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–08 | Brief + safety | State the purpose of the practical, the expected outcome, and the safety/kit rules. In a lab, this is non-negotiable. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 08–18 | Pre-lab thinking | BEFORE touching the equipment/software: predict what should happen, write the method in your own words, draw the setup. | Activities: Prediction |
| 18–48 | The practical | The doing. You circulate with a checklist, and note students' confidence. Intervene for safety or stuck-ness, not for every error. | Activities |
| 48–55 | Results + sense-making | Record results; graph them; write one sentence of conclusion. The data is not the lesson — interpreting it is. | Worksheets |
| 55–60 | Link back to theory | Tie the practical to the theory it illustrates. Ask: did it match your prediction? Why/why not? | Reflection |

**Decision points to make yourself:** what the *learning objective* of the practical is (the kit and the fun can obscure it); how much scaffolding the pre-lab thinking needs; the safety briefing content specific to your setting.

**Why this template is different:** the most common failure in practical lessons is that students do the procedure and learn nothing. The prediction step and the sense-making step are the parts that make it educational — guard them.

## Template 6 — Project-based session

**Use when:** a multi-session project — a research essay, a build, a presentation, a short film, a portfolio piece. This template is for one working session within a longer arc, so it needs the project's overall shape to exist first.

**Top line:** Project: `{{project}}` | Session number: `{{session_number}}` of `{{total_sessions}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Student(s): `{{student_or_class}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Anchor to the project | Where are we in the project, what did we complete last time, what is due at the end. Agree this session's sub-goal. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 05–15 | Skills input | One 10-minute chunk of the specific skill this session needs (structuring an argument, citing sources, a tool technique). Teach only what the project needs now. | Activities |
| 15–50 | Working block | The project work itself. In a group, roles are assigned; you check in at set points and give feedback on the work, not the person. | Activities: Project work |
| 50–58 | Progress check | Student self-reports: what's done, what's stuck, what's next. You record the sticking points for next session. | Progress Reports |
| 58–60 | Close + next step | Agree the concrete deliverable for next session and what the student must prepare before it. | Homework & Follow-up |

**Decision points to make yourself:** the project's assessment criteria (what does "done well" mean — write it down at the start); how you will keep the project on track across many sessions; what to do when a student goes off-brief — this is a coaching decision, not an AI one.

**Why this template is different:** project sessions live in a longer arc, so each session must build on the last and set up the next. The "anchor to the project" phase is what keeps a series of sessions from drifting.

## Template 7 — Assessment day

**Use when:** a full session dedicated to assessing — a mock, a term test, a progress check, or a diagnostic baseline. The line between assessment and teaching should be deliberate: on an assessment day you are collecting evidence, not teaching.

**Top line:** Assessment: `{{assessment_name}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Purpose: diagnostic / termly / mock (`{{purpose}}`) | Length: `{{length}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Set up | Explain the format, the time, the rules, and what will happen with the results. Reduce anxiety, don't add to it. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 05–[end] | The assessment | The student completes it under the agreed conditions. You may observe process (but not interfere). No teaching during the assessment. | Quizzes & Assessment |
| [end]–+10 | Immediate light de-brief | A very light, encouraging "how did that feel" — NO marking in the session. Capture their self-assessment. | Reflection |
| Later | Marking + analysis | You mark and diagnose separately (see the Analysis prompt in Quizzes & Assessment). | Quizzes: Analysis |

**Decision points to make yourself:** what the *purpose* is (diagnostic vs summative changes what you can conclude); whether to allow notes/calculators (this must be decided before, and written on the paper); how to handle the emotional weight — an assessment a student feels was "awful" is still data.

**Why this template is different:** the discipline of an assessment day is *not teaching*. If you find yourself explaining a question mid-assessment, you have changed the assessment's meaning. Record the question, mark it afterwards, and teach it next lesson.

## Template 8 — Catch-up / remedial session

**Use when:** a student has a specific gap — missed topics, a shaky foundation, a concept they never quite got. The opposite of the mainline lesson: you are rebuilding, not extending. Nothing here assumes prior knowledge; everything checks it.

**Top line:** Student: `{{student_name}}` | Subject: `{{subject}}` | The gap: `{{specific_gap}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | What they DO know: `{{prior_knowledge}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–10 | Find the floor | A very small diagnostic (5 targeted questions) to find exactly where the understanding breaks. Is it this topic, or something 3 topics back? | Quizzes: Diagnostic |
| 10–20 | Repair the foundation | Teach only the broken piece, at the level below the expected one if needed. Use a concrete example from the student's world. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 20–40 | Overlearn | Lots of practice, but at a pace the student can actually keep. Success builds confidence; confidence is the point here. | Activities: Guided practice |
| 40–52 | Bridge forward | Now connect the repaired foundation to the topic they were supposed to learn. Show that the old gap is closed. | Activities: Application |
| 52–60 | Consolidate + next | A final check that the foundation holds, and a note of what's now possible. Set a confidence-building homework. | Homework & Follow-up |

**Decision points to make yourself:** whether the gap is truly in this topic or upstream of it (test upstream first); the emotional register — remedial sessions are where self-belief is won or lost, pace to success; what "good enough for now" means, so you don't over-teach a foundation that only needed a patch.

**Why this template is different:** remedial work is not slower mainline teaching; it is targeted repair. The floor-finding step is the most important and the most skipped — never assume the gap is where it appears to be.

## Template 9 — Exam-style mock

**Use when:** a full-length practice exam under timed conditions, usually close to the real exam. This is the highest-stakes template; it is about realism, stamina and calibration.

**Top line:** Exam: `{{exam}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Paper: `{{paper}}` | Time available: `{{time}}` | Marks: `{{marks}}`

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| 00–10 | Briefing | Explain exactly what the real exam will feel like, what to do if stuck, and how marks are allocated. No content teaching. | Lesson Plan Design |
| 10–[end of paper] | The mock | Real conditions: timed, closed-book (or as the real exam), no help. You observe technique (watching the clock, skipping and returning, showing working). | Quizzes: Exam-style |
| [end]–+15 | Immediate self-mark | Student marks their own paper against a mark scheme — this is a powerful learning moment that also saves you time. Capture their self-assessment. | Quizzes: Analysis |
| After | Full mark + diagnostic | Produce the mock report: score, grade, and a ranked list of gaps. The parent report for this is in Progress Reports. | Progress Reports |

**Decision points to make yourself:** choosing the right paper (past paper for the exact board, or your own compiled paper); whether the mock is formative (learning) or summative (a grade) — state it before they sit it; how to frame the result so the student leaves motivated, not crushed — the report does this work.

**Why this template is different:** the value of a mock is the *experience of the conditions* plus a precise diagnostic, not the score. Never let the score become the headline of the session — the gap list is the headline.

## Template 10 — Lesson from a messy source material

**Use when:** the source material is not a tidy textbook — a long article, a textbook chapter with too much detail, a YouTube video, a podcast, a set of handwritten notes from a missing class, dense slides. The skill here is *curation*: turning messy input into a structured lesson.

**Top line:** Source: `{{source_type}}` | Topic: `{{topic}}` | Level: `{{level}}` | Student(s): `{{student_or_class}}` | Problem with source: `{{problem}}` (too long / too dense / too advanced / incomplete)

| Time | Phase | What happens | Generate with |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Curate | Use the "messy source" prompt to extract the 3–5 ideas that matter, the key vocabulary, and the single best example. The AI's job is to carve the lesson out of the source. | Lesson Plan Design: messy source |
| 00–08 | Hooking the idea | Start not with the source but with a question the source answers. Give the student a reason to read. | Activities: Starter |
| 08–30 | Work the source | The lesson works *through* the source (a guided reading, a structured note-taking, a "why does this matter" conversation) — you are not teaching the source, the source is teaching the student. | Worksheets |
| 30–45 | Apply | A task that uses the ideas from the source in a fresh context, proving transfer. | Activities: Application |
| 45–55 | Synthesise | Student writes a 3-bullet summary of the source in their own words — this is the proof they understood it, not just read it. | Quizzes |
| 55–60 | Connect | Link the source to the wider topic and to what comes next. | Reflection |

**Decision points to make yourself:** what to *cut* (the AI can help you see what's in the source, but you decide what the student actually needs); how much of the source the student should read directly vs. your summary — reading stamina is part of the curriculum too; what the "single best example" is — the one that makes the idea click.

**Why this template is different:** most real lesson planning time is spent here — wrestling with a bad chapter, a rambling video, a dense paper. This template mechanises the curation so you spend your time on the coaching, not the formatting.

## Lesson Plan Design

### Prompt 1. The complete lesson plan from scratch

```
You are an expert classroom teacher and tutor with 15 years of experience in {{subject}} at {{level}} level.

Write a complete lesson plan for a {{duration}} lesson on {{topic}} for a {{student_or_class}}.
The student's current level in this area is {{current_level}}; their target is {{target_level}}.

The lesson must include:
1. One precise learning objective (what the student will be able to do by the end).
2. A 3-minute "do now" retrieval starter.
3. A teaching chunk (max 12 minutes) with a clear explanation of the key idea and one worked example.
4. Guided practice with 3-5 problems/tasks, with model answers.
5. Independent practice with 2-3 problems/tasks.
6. A check-for-understanding question.
7. An extension task for fast finishers.
8. A homework prompt the student can complete in {{homework_minutes}} minutes.
9. A "common misconception" note — what the student will probably get wrong and how to pre-empt it.
10. A differentiation note for a student who is {{differentiation_profile}}.

Output format:
- A table with columns: Time | Phase | What the tutor does | What the student does.
- Above the table, the learning objective and success criteria.
- Below the table, the misconception note, the extension and the homework.
- Use plain, direct language. Do not add generic praise or filler.
```

**How to use:** the workhorse of the pack — use it when you have a topic and nothing else. It produces a full skeleton you then refine. Check the misconception note carefully; it is often the most valuable part.

### Prompt 2. Turn a topic into a lesson sequence

```
You are a curriculum designer. I need to teach {{topic}} over {{number_of_lessons}} lessons to a {{level}} {{student_or_class}} whose current level is {{current_level}}.

Plan the sequence of the {{number_of_lessons}} lessons. For each lesson give:
1. A title.
2. The one learning objective.
3. The "hook" or starter for the lesson.
4. The core activity (1 line).
5. What must be remediated from the previous lesson before moving on.
6. The homework that connects to the next lesson.

The sequence must follow the principle: teach → practice → check → extend, and must build each lesson on the last. The final lesson in the sequence should assess the whole set of objectives.

Output: a markdown table, one row per lesson, plus one short paragraph explaining the logic of the order (why this sequence, not another).
```

**How to use:** for planning a half-term or a multi-week run rather than a single session — e.g. "teach fractions over 6 lessons". The final,paragraph explaining the sequencing logic lets you sanity-check the AI's reasoning.

### Prompt 3. Fill one phase of an existing lesson

```
You are an expert tutor. I am planning a {{phase}} for a {{subject}} lesson at {{level}} level.
The topic is {{topic}}; the learning objective is {{objective}}. The student is {{age_profile}} and learns best via {{learning_style}}.

Generate the {{phase}} content:
- Starter / teach / guided practice / independent practice / plenary / homework (choose one when you fill this in).

The {{phase}} must:
- Take {{time_allotted}} minutes.
- Match the objective exactly (no tangents).
- Be pitched at {{current_level}}.
- Include concrete, real examples, not abstract ones.
- Provide the answers or a mark scheme.
- Include a 2-line instruction I can say aloud to the student.

Output: the content, ready to use, with a short note on the most likely sticking point for this student.
```

**How to use:** when you only need one chunk of a lesson (e.g. the starter or the independent practice), not the whole thing. Faster and more precise than regenerating the full plan.

### Prompt 4. Convert a textbook / source into a lesson

```
You are an expert tutor in {{subject}}. Here is a source on {{topic}}:

---SOURCE START---
{{paste_the_source_or_share_a_link}}
---SOURCE END---

I need to teach the key ideas from this source to a {{level}} student in a {{duration}} lesson.
The student's level is {{current_level}} and their main difficulty is {{difficulty}}.

Do the following:
1. Extract the 3-5 essential ideas — the ones a {{level}} student must understand. Ignore the rest.
2. List the key vocabulary terms and give a one-line student-friendly definition for each.
3. Identify the one best example or analogy from the source to anchor the lesson on.
4. Flag anything in the source that is wrong, out of date, or misleading for this level.
5. Build a lesson outline around these essentials with timings, and set a homework task derived from the source.

If the source is too long for the lesson, tell me what to cut and why.
Output: markdown with clear sections (Essential ideas, Vocabulary, Anchor example, Corrections, Lesson plan, Homework).
```

**How to use:** this is the "messy source" template in prompt form — use it for chapters, articles, videos, slides, notes. The correction step is gold: have the AI flag anything dubious before you teach it.

### Prompt 5. Lesson plan for a mixed-level group

```
You are an expert tutor teaching a group of {{group_size}} students at slightly different levels in {{subject}}.
```

### Prompt 6. Design a lesson around a single misconception

## Learning Objectives & Assessment

### Prompt 8. Write precise, measurable learning objectives

```
You are an expert in writing learning objectives. I teach {{subject}} at {{level}} level.

Write {{number}} learning objectives for a lesson on {{topic}}, for a student at {{current_level}} heading to {{target_level}}.

Each objective must:
1. Start with an action verb that is measurable and observable (e.g. "solve", "explain", "compare", "construct", "evaluate" — not "understand" or "know").
2. State the condition (what the student will have available — "without a calculator", "from a source", "in 10 minutes").
3. State the success criterion (how we will know they have it — "to at least 80% accuracy", "using all three sources", "with correct units").
4. Be pitched at the student's current level, not the aspirational level.
5. Include at least one objective at a higher-order thinking level (analyse / evaluate / create).

Then give me a single-sentence "today's outcome" I can say aloud to the student at the start of the lesson.
Output: numbered objectives, then the sentence. No filler.
```

**How to use:** weak objectives ("understand fractions") produce weak lessons. This forces measurable verbs and success criteria — and the "say aloud" sentence is a genuinely useful habit.

### Prompt 9. Turn objectives into success criteria and an exit ticket

```

2. For each success criterion, write the exact thing I should look for to decide "yes, met" vs "not yet".
3. Write an exit-ticket question (2 questions maximum) that maps to the success criteria and lets me judge in under 2 minutes whether the objective landed.
4. Write a short "if they can't do this, then..." guidance — for each criterion, what it implies for next lesson.

Output: a table with columns Success criterion | What I look for | If not met, next step | plus the exit ticket questions.
```

**How to use:** pairs with any lesson. The "if not met, next step" column turns a checklist into a diagnostic that plans your next session.

# One Lesson, End to End — a worked 60-minute maths example

Theory is easier than practice, so here is one complete lesson built purely from this pack. It shows the actual prompt choices, the actual placeholder-filling, and the actual output at each step — then the final lesson. The example is maths (linear equations, KS4/GCSE level) but the *process* is identical for any subject.

**The scenario:** I have a single GCSE student, Amy, working at a grade 4 (of 9). She can solve simple two-step equations (`3x + 2 = 11`) but keeps dropping terms when the unknown appears on both sides (`5x − 3 = 2x + 9`). I have a 60-minute 1-to-1 session. Her target is grade 5.

### Step 0 — I set my teaching brief

I keep a one-page brief for Amy (built with Prompt 53). Key lines:

- Level: GCSE Maths, currently grade 4, targeting grade 5.
- Difficulty: drops terms when the unknown is on both sides; also mixes up the order of inverse operations.
- Style: value understanding over memorising; I want her to "check by substituting back".
- Never: generic praise, unsupported claims, worksheets that can't be printed.

### Step 1 — I pick a template

I choose **Template 3 (1-to-1 tutoring session)** — it's a single student, 60 minutes, a known specific gap. That gives me the armature: Connect → Precise target → Teach + go deep → Application → Consolidation → Set next.

### Step 2 — I diagnose the real gap

Instead of trusting my assumption, I run **Prompt 12 (diagnostic)** with Amy for 5 minutes. She answers 5 quick questions. Result: she solves `5x − 3 = 2x + 9` and *does* collect the `x` terms correctly when they're collected to the left, but she writes `2x + 9 − 2x = 5x − 3` and then cancels the wrong way. The real gap is **collecting like terms correctly on the correct side and preserving the equation's balance** — not what I assumed. The lesson now has a sharper target.

### Step 3 — I write the objective

Using **Prompt 8 (learning objectives)**, I settle on:

> **Objective:** By the end of the session, Amy will solve linear equations with the unknown on both sides by collecting like terms onto one side and applying inverse operations in the correct order, checking her answer by substitution, to 100% accuracy on 4 consecutive independent attempts.
>
> **Say-aloud sentence:** "By the end of today you'll be able to solve any equation where the letter is on both sides — and you'll know you're right because you can check it."

### Step 4 — I generate the lesson content

I feed **Prompt 1 (complete lesson plan)** with my filled-in specifics: subject = maths, level = GCSE, topic = linear equations with unknown on both sides, duration = 60 min, student = Amy, current level = grade 4 (weak on like-terms collection), target = grade 5, differentiation note = "needs a visual balance model and step-by-step scaffolding".

The AI returns a structured plan. I *verify its maths* — every equation balances, every inverse step is correct — and I tighten the timing to the diagnostic I already ran. Here is the refined plan:

| Time | Phase | What I do | What Amy does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Connect + recall | Check homework; ask "what was the rule for two-step equations?" | Answers; reveals she's more confident than last week |
| 05–10 | Precise target | Write today's objective + the say-aloud sentence on the board | Reads it back; nods; says "I keep messing up the letters" |
| 10–18 | Teach — the balance model | Use a simple balance/scale analogy. Show `5x − 3 = 2x + 9` on the scale; physically "remove" `2x` from both sides, then show why we subtract 2x from *both* sides. Model one worked example aloud, narrating every step | Watches; answers "what happens to the scale if I take 2x off only one side?" |
| 18–32 | Guided practice (we do) | Two problems together. Amy says each step; I only prompt, don't do it. We write both a correct and an "I accidentally..." version and compare | Solves two equations aloud, narrating the balance step |
| 32–45 | Application (she does) | Three independent problems, increasing difficulty, including one with `x` appearing as a negative coefficient | Solves them. I watch her process, not just answers |
| 45–52 | Consolidation | Amy writes the rule in her own words (3 lines) and solves one final problem completely alone | Writes the rule and solves the final check problem |
| 52–60 | Set next | We mark the final problem together; she checks by substitution. Agree homework (Prompt 32) and the error-log entry (Prompt 56) | Substitutes her answer back in; confirms it works; agrees to log the "dropping terms" slip if it reappears |

### Step 5 — I generate the supporting materials

- **Practice set** (Prompt 15 scaffolded, and Prompt 16 independent): I get a 5-problem set with model answers, hints and a "challenge" final problem. I check every answer.

**Which AI tools does this work with?**
Any modern chat-based AI that accepts detailed prompts — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and similar. The prompts are tool-agnostic. Best results come from tools that handle long, structured instructions well.

**How is this different from just asking an AI to "write a lesson plan"?**
Three ways. (1) The prompts set a role, audience, constraints and output format, so results are consistently structured and usable. Not automatically — and that's true of any AI-generated content. **Is this really 50+ prompts?**

**How do the PDF and Notion formats work?**
You receive both. The PDF is for reading offline and printing the sections you use most. The Notion version imports the whole pack as clean, editable pages (one page per prompt), so you can fill prompts in place, keep a database of lessons, and build your own library over time.

**What if a prompt doesn't fit my exact student?**
