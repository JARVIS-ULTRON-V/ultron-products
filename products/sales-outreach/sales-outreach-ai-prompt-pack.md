## How to use this pack

This pack is not a single prompt you paste and forget. It's a system. Here's how to get value from it in under 15 minutes:

1. **Fill in your {{placeholders}} honestly.** Every prompt has placeholders like {{prospect_name}}, {{research_notes}}, and {{specific_trigger}}. The person you're writing to can feel a placeholder that was filled with a guess. Invest in the research step (Section: Personalization layer) and your reply rate will do the talking.
2. **Pick the sequence that matches the situation.** Scanning 15 sequences is quicker than reinventing a cadence every time. Match the scenario — cold first touch, pricing objection, no reply ever — and adapt.
3. **Run prompts through a 30-second quality gate.** Before you send anything, check it against the "Rules that make emails land" section. If it violates a rule, fix it before it ships.
4. **Use the prompts as scaffolding, not output.** The point is to *write* the email, not to have AI write it for you. Use these prompts to get 90% of the way, then edit for your voice and the specific prospect's reality.
5. **Track what works.** Note which sequences and touches get replies. The sequences here are proven starting points, not magic. Test and iterate.

The format is PDF + Notion. You can copy any block into Notion, keep your own sequences, and manage your pipeline from one place.

## Rules that make emails land

These are the non-negotiables. They are the difference between spam and a reply. Print this section.

1. **Under 120 words.** A cold email that runs past 120 words is a wall of text. Short emails get read. Long emails get archived. If it's over, cut.
2. **One ask, one email.** Never ask for a meeting *and* a reply *and* a referral in the same email. Decide the single action you want and make it the only CTA.
3. **No AI tells.** If an email sounds like a robot wrote it, it will be treated like a robot. Avoid "I hope this email finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," "I'm reaching out because," "game-changer," "just wanted to touch base," "synergy," "circle back." Read your draft aloud. If it sounds like a corporate memo, rewrite it.
4. **No fake familiarity.** You don't know the prospect. Don't write "I loved your recent post" if you didn't read it. Don't pretend you've been following their career for years. Authenticity wins.
5. **Specificity beats flattery.** "I noticed you're hiring for a VP of Sales" is specific. "Your company is doing great things" is filler. Specificity is the currency of a cold email.
6. **Personalize the reason, not just the name.** Using the prospect's name is table stakes. The real personalization is showing you understand a specific thing about their business, role, or recent activity.
7. **The CTA must be low-friction.** "Do you have 15 minutes next week?" is a request for a decision. "Can I send you a one-paragraph summary?" is lower friction. The lower the friction, the higher the reply rate.
8. **Make it mobile-first.** Most cold emails are read on a phone. Keep paragraphs short (2–3 sentences max), use line breaks, and keep your total message scannable.
9. **Answer: why now?** Every email needs a reason to act *now*, not later. A trigger ("I saw you just raised a Series B"), a deadline, or a time-sensitive offer. Without it, your email goes to "later" (which is never).
10. **Respect the follow-up cadence.** One email is a message. Two emails is a conversation. Don't fire off five identical emails. Each touch must add new value — a new insight, a new angle, a new reason to reply. (See the No Reply Ever sequence.)

## Sequence 1: Cold First Touch

**Touches: 3** | **Duration: ~9 days**

The classic opening sequence for a brand-new cold relationship. It starts with a single, trigger-based email, then two value-adding follow-ups.

| Touch | Timing | Channel | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Day 1 | Email | The first, personalized cold email with a specific trigger |
| 2 | Day 4 | LinkedIn (connection request) | Adds a social layer without being pushy |
| 3 | Day 7 | Email | A short, new-value follow-up (not a repeat) |

### Touch 1 — The first email

```
Subject: {{specific_trigger}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

I noticed {{specific_trigger}} (e.g., you just raised a $10M Series A / you're hiring for a {{role}} / you posted about {{topic}}).

I work with {{similar_company_or_role}} on {{problem_you_solve}}, and {{proof_point_or_result}}.

Would it be useful if I sent you a 2-minute read on {{one_thing_related_to_their_goal}}?

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** It opens with a specific trigger, not a greeting. It immediately positions you as relevant. The CTA is low-friction (send a read, not a meeting).

### Touch 2 — LinkedIn connection request

```
Hi {{prospect_name}} — saw {{specific_trigger}}, thought it was worth connecting. I'm {{your_name}}, {{your_role}} at {{your_company}}. Always keen to meet people working on {{their_space}}.
```

**Why it works:** It's a soft touch that puts you on their radar without demanding a reply. It doesn't ask for anything — it just connects.

### Touch 3 — The follow-up email

```
Subject: Re: {{orignal_subject}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

Following up on my note from last week. I know you're busy, so let me keep this short.

The one thing that surprises every founder I talk to: {{surprising_stat_or_insight}}.

If it's relevant, I can send you the full breakdown — just hit reply and I'll send it over.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** It adds a new insight (not a repeat of the first email). It gives a reason to reply ("hit reply and I'll send it") that's easier than booking a call.

## Sequence 2: 3-Touch Follow-Up

**Touches: 3** | **Duration: ~14 days**

Use this when the prospect has seen your first email but hasn't replied. The strategy: keep adding value, never repeat yourself.

| Touch | Timing | Channel | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Day 1 | Email | Initial personalized email |
| 2 | Day 4 | Email | Value-add follow-up with a new insight |
| 3 | Day 7 | Email | Final, low-friction ask + a soft out |

### Touch 1 — Initial email

```
Subject: Quick question about {{their_initiative}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

I saw {{specific_trigger}} and I'm curious about {{one_aspect_of_their_work}}.

We help {{similar_company_or_role}} do {{problem_you_solve}}, and recently {{proof_point_or_result}}.

If you're open to it, I can send over a short breakdown of how we do {{that_thing}} — no call needed.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 2 — Add value

```
Subject: Re: {{original_subject}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

In case it's useful: I put together a quick look at {{topic_or_insight}} that's relevant to what you're doing with {{their_initiative}}.

Here's the one-line version: {{insight_one_liner}}.

Happy to share the full thing if you want it — just reply and I'll send it.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 3 — The soft close

```
Subject: Re: {{original_subject}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

I know this might not be a priority right now, and that's fine.

If {{their_initiative_in is something you're actively thinking about, I'd be glad to help. If not, no worries at all — I won't keep following up.

Either way, I'd love to know — is this on your radar?

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

**Why it's the final touch:** It gives the prospect an easy out, which paradoxically makes it easier to reply. The question "is this on your radar?" is low-stakes and gets a yes/no reply.

## Sequence 3: Re-Engagement

**Touches: 3** | **Duration: ~10 days**

For prospects who went cold — they replied once, went quiet, or stopped responding mid-conversation. The goal is to restart a stalled conversation, not re-sell.

| Touch | Timing | Channel | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Day 1 | Email | Acknowledgment + a low-pressure check-in |
| 2 | Day 4 | Email | A "what changed" value add |
| 3 | Day 8 | Email | A final, honest ask |

### Touch 1 — The check-in

```
Subject: Checking in on {{topic}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

A few weeks ago we were talking about {{topic}}, and I wanted to check in — did that go anywhere?

If your priorities have shifted, completely understand. If not, happy to pick up where we left off.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 2 — What changed

```
Subject: A quick update on {{topic}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

Since we last spoke, a couple of things changed on our end: {{what_changed}}.

I thought you might want to know, given where we left off with {{topic}}.

Worth a quick chat to catch up? No pressure.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 3 — The honest close

```
Subject: Re: {{original_subject}}

Hi {{prospect_name}},

I'll keep this brief — I don't want to be a pest.

If {{topic}} is something you're still potentially interested in, I'm here. If it's gone quiet and that's the answer, that's fine too — just let me know and I'll stop.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** It treats the prospect like a human, gives them an out, and makes the "not interested" answer easy. That honesty gets replies that silence doesn't.

## Sequence 4: Referral Ask

Ask a satisfied client or a connection for a referral — without being awkward.

|-------|--------|---------|---------|

```
Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 2 — Follow-up

```
Subject: Re: A favor?
```

## Sequence 5: Pricing Objection

**Touches: 2** | **Duration: ~6 days**

Your prospect's concern is price. You need to reframe value, not discount.

| Touch | Timing | Channel | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Day 1 | Email | Reframe the ROI, acknowledge the concern |
| 2 | Day 4 | Email | Offer a smaller entry point or a trial |

### Touch 1 — Reframe ROI

```
Subject: On pricing

Hi {{prospect_name}},

Fair point on the price. Let me give you the math.

Most of our clients in {{industry}} spend {{$X}} a month on {{problem_you_solve}}, and we typically help them save {{$Y}} or make {{$Z}} within {{timeframe}}.

So the question isn't "is it expensive" — it's "what does it cost you *not* to have it?"

Happy to walk you through the numbers for your situation.

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

### Touch 2 — Entry point

```
Subject: Re: On pricing

Hi {{prospect_name}},

I get it — a big commitment is a big decision.

We also have a {{smaller_package_or_trial}} that gets you {{one_concrete_outcome}} for a fraction of the cost. It's a way to de-risk the decision.

Want me to send over the details?

Best,
{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** It never discounts the value — it reframes it. Then it offers a lower-friction entry that keeps the deal alive.

```

```

### Touch 3 — Second stakeholder


## Cold Email Foundations

### Prompt 1: The Trigger-First Cold Email

```
You are a sales professional writing a cold email to {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}, who is a {{role}}.

Start with the single most specific, relevant thing you know about them: {{specific_trigger}} (a recent hire, a funding round, a post they wrote, a product launch).

Then connect that trigger to the problem you solve: {{problem_you_solve}}.

Then give one credible proof point: {{proof_point_or_result}}.

End with ONE low-friction ask: {{cta}} (e.g., "Can I send you a 2-minute read?").

Rules:
- Under 120 words.
- No "I hope this email finds you well" or "I wanted to reach out."
- No fake familiarity.
- One ask only.
- Specific, concrete language. No fluff.
```

**How to use:** This is your master template. Run it for every new prospect, changing only the placeholders.

### Prompt 2: The Problem-Agitation Email

```
Write a cold email to {{prospect_name}}, a {{role}} at {{company}}.

Structure:
1. Subject: A question about {{problem_you_solve}}.
2. Opening: Name the specific problem they likely face: {{specific_problem}}.
3. Agitate: Show the cost of ignoring it: {{cost_of_inaction}}.
4. Solution: Briefly introduce how you help: {{your_solution}}.
5. CTA: One low-friction ask: {{cta}}.

Rules:
- Under 120 words.
- The problem must be specific to their industry/role, not generic.
- Don't oversell. State the problem, then your offer.
- End with one clear ask.
```

**How to use:** Use when you have a clear read on the prospect's pain but limited personal data. The agitation step drives urgency.

### Prompt 3: The Case-Study Cold Email

```
Write a cold email to {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}.

Lead with a relevant case study or result: {{case_study}} (e.g., "We helped a company like yours cut {{metric}} by {{X}}% in {{timeframe}}.").

Explain why this is relevant to {{company}}: {{relevance}}.

Offer to share the full story: {{cta}}.

Rules:
- Under 120 words.
- The case study must be real and credible.
- Connect the case study explicitly to the prospect's situation.
- One ask only.
```

**How to use:** Use when you have a strong, relevant case study. Lead with the outcome, not with yourself.

### Prompt 4: The Benchmarked/Stat Cold Email

```
Write a cold email to {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}.

Open with a surprising or relevant stat or benchmark: {{stat_or_benchmark}} (related to {{problem_you_solve}}).

Make it feel relevant to them: {{why_relevant}}.

Then offer your solution + a single CTA: {{solution_and_cta}}.

Rules:
- Under 120 words.
- The stat must be true and sourced (don't invent data).
- Make it personal to the prospect, not generic.
- One ask.
```

**How to use:** Use when you have a strong industry stat that highlights the problem. Make sure it's credible — a made-up stat kills trust.

### Prompt 5: The One-Paragraph Value Statement

```
Write a one-paragraph value statement for {{your_company}} aimed at {{prospect_name}}, a {{role}} at {{company}}.

The statement must answer, in order:
1. Who you help: {{customer_type}}.
2. What problem you solve: {{problem_you_solve}}.
3. How you solve it (the mechanism): {{how_you_solve_it}}.
4. The proof: {{proof_point_or_result}}.

Keep it under 100 words. Use plain, concrete language. No hype words (game-changer, revolutionary, best-in-class, cutting-edge).
```

**How to use:** Use this to craft the core value proposition that powers every email and DM. Perfect it once, reuse everywhere.

### Prompt 6: The Rewrite-This-Cold-Email Prompt

```
Here is a cold email draft that isn't working:

"""
{{your_current_email}}
"""

Rewrite it to follow these rules:
1. Under 120 words.
2. Remove all clichés and AI tells ("I hope this email finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," "game-changer").
3. Replace generic statements with specific ones based on {{research_notes}}.
4. Make the CTA singular and low-friction.
5. Open with the most specific, relevant trigger.

Output the rewritten email, then briefly list what you changed and why.
```

**How to use:** Run your existing, underperforming emails through this. It's the fastest way to fix sloppy outreach.

## Personalization & Research

### Prompt 7: Research-Signal Extracter

```
Here are the notes from a prospect's LinkedIn, website, and recent activity: {{raw_prospect_notes}}.

Extract the 5 strongest personalization signals:
1. The most specific, recent trigger (hiring, funding, launch, post).
2. A specific problem they likely face, given their role.
3. A concrete detail about their company (size, stage, industry, tech stack).
4. A shared connection or common ground, if any.
5. A specific, relevant way we could help.

Format the output as a clean list under the heading "RESEARCH NOTES" — ready to paste into a cold email template.
```

**How to use:** Paste raw research into this prompt before writing any email. It forces you to find the real signals, not the obvious ones.

### Prompt 8: The Triggers List

```
Brainstorm 10 specific, genuine triggers that would make {{prospect_name}}, a {{role}} at {{company}}, a good time to talk to {{your_company}}.

Triggers look like:
- A recent hire, funding round, or product launch.
- A post they wrote or shared.
- A press mention or award.
- A job posting that suggests a problem.
- A change in their strategy or positioning.

For each trigger, write one sentence on why it's relevant to {{problem_you_solve}}.

Output as a numbered list.
```

**How to use:** Build a bank of triggers for each persona. Use the best one as the opening of your cold email.

### Prompt 9: Connect-the-Dots Personalization

```
I sell {{your_offer}} to {{target_persona}}.

Here is what I found about {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}: {{research_notes}}.

Create a personalized opening line that connects a specific detail from the research to the problem I solve. It must:
- Reference something concrete and true about them.
- Make a genuine, non-cringe link to {{problem_you_solve}}.
- Be under 40 words.

Give me 3 options, each a different angle (e.g., a compliment on a post, an observation about their hiring, a question about their strategy).
```

**How to use:** This generates the hard part — the personalized opener. Pick the most natural option, don't just take the first one.

### Prompt 10: The "So What" Personalization

```
I'm writing to {{prospect_name}}, a {{role}} at {{company}}.

Here's the fact I want to use: {{research_notes}}.

That fact alone is not enough. Generate the "so what" — why does this fact mean they might care about {{problem_you_solve}}?

Write 3 versions, each connecting the fact to a different angle of the problem. Each must be specific and credible, not a stretch.

Format:
Fact: {{fact}}
Implication 1: ...
Implication 2: ...
Implication 3: ...
```

**How to use:** This turns a bare fact into a reason to care. Use it whenever your personalization feels flat.

### Prompt 11: Common-Ground Finder

```
Find genuine common ground between me and {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}.

My background: {{your_background}} (schools, companies, causes, cities, interests).
They studied/worked at: {{prospect_background}}.
Company details: {{company_details}}.

Identify up to 3 genuine commonalities (shared alma mater, previous employer, industry, city, cause, interest). For each, write a natural one-line way to reference it in a cold email without being creepy or fake.

If there's no genuine common ground, say so — do not force one.
```

**How to use:** Use sparingly. A real shared connection is powerful; a forced one reads as manipulation. If there's nothing, skip it.

### Prompt 12: 5-Angle Personalization

```
For {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}, generate 5 different personalization angles for a cold email:

1. The recent event angle (hiring, funding, launch, news).
2. The content angle (a post, article, or talk they published).
3. The role angle (something specific about their job responsibilities).
4. The company angle (a company metric, product, or positioning detail).
5. The network angle (a shared connection or mutual context).

For each angle, write a one-sentence opener that's specific, true, and relevant to {{problem_you_solve}}.

Output as a numbered list.
```

**How to use:** Generate all five, then pick the strongest. This ensures you're not defaulting to the same angle every time.

## Subject Lines

### Prompt 13: The Subject-Line Generator

```
Generate 10 subject lines for a cold email to {{prospect_name}} at {{company}} about {{topic}}.

Rules:
- Under 8 words each.
- No spammy words (free, urgent, guaranteed, 100%, act now).
- No "I hope this finds you well."
- Must be specific and curiosity-driven or clear about the value.
- Vary the style: curiosity, question, benefit, trigger-based, reference to a specific thing.

Output as a numbered list. Then bold the 3 you think are strongest and say why.
```

**How to use:** Run this for every new email, then A/B test the top two. Subject lines are worth investing in — they decide the open.

### Prompt 14: The Subject-Line Matrix

```
I need subject lines across several angles for an email to {{prospect_name}}.

Create a markdown table with these columns: Style | Subject Line | Why it works.

Cover these styles:
1. Trigger-based (references something specific about them).
2. Question (opens a question they want answered).
3. Benefit (names the outcome).
4. Curiosity (creates an open loop).
5. Low-effort reply (invites a one-word answer).
6. Personalized reference (references a specific detail).

Give at least 2 lines per style.
```

**How to use:** Keep this matrix as a reference. Pick the style that fits the situation and the audience.

### Prompt 15: Subject-Line Fixer

```
Here are some subject lines that aren't performing: {{current_subject_lines}}.

Rewrite them to follow these rules:
1. Under 8 words.
2. Specific, not generic.
3. No spam triggers.
4. Earn the open — say something true and useful or intriguing.
5. Match the tone of the email body.

For each, give the original and the rewrite, plus a one-line reason.
```

**How to use:** When opens are low, run your existing subject lines through this. It's a fast diagnosis too.

### Prompt 16: The Curiosity-Gap Subject Line

```
Write 6 subject lines that create a curiosity gap for {{prospect_name}}.

A curiosity gap promises useful information without revealing all of it. Examples of the shape:
- "The {{X}} most people miss"
- "What {{metric}} actually means for {{company}}"
- "A faster way to {{outcome}}"

Rules:
- Under 8 words.
- Must be honest — the body must deliver on the promise.
- Must be relevant to {{topic}} and {{problem_you_solve}}.
- No clickbait or misleading promises.
```

**How to use:** Curiosity gaps get opens, but only if the email delivers. Never use a subject line the body doesn't justify.

### Prompt 17: Reply-Generating Subject Line

```
Write 6 subject lines engineered to invite a short, easy reply from {{prospect_name}}.

These should make it trivial to answer "yes" or "no" or a one-liner. Examples of the shape:
- "Quick question about {{topic}}"
- "Worth a look?"
- "Is {{problem}} on your radar?"

Rules:
- Under 7 words.
- Must clearly be a question that's easy to answer.
- Must be relevant and not pushy.
- The email body must end with a matching low-friction ask.
```

**How to use:** Use these when your goal is a reply (not necessarily a meeting). They convert to conversations, which you can then steer.

### Prompt 18: The Familiar-Sender Subject Line

```
Write 6 subject lines that make {{prospect_name}} feel the email is worth opening because it looks familiar, useful, or connected — without being deceptive.

Ideas to work from:
- Referencing a shared context: "Following up on {{event}}"
- Referencing a resource: "The {{resource}} you requested"
- Referencing a connection: "Re: {{mutual_contact}}"

Rules:
- Must be truthful. Never imply a relationship that doesn't exist.
- Under 8 words.
- Produce 6 options with a note on when to use each.
```

**How to use:** Use only when the reference is real. Faking familiarity is fast reputational damage.

## LinkedIn DMs

### Prompt 19: The LinkedIn Connection Request

```
Write a LinkedIn connection request to {{prospect_name}}, a {{role}} at {{company}}.

Rules:
- Under 300 characters (LinkedIn's limit).
- Must be specific and relevant, not generic.
- Reference {{specific_trigger}} or a genuine reason to connect.
- No pitching in the request itself. No "I'd love to pick your brain."
- End with nothing that demands a reply — just a clean reason to connect.

Give me 3 options with slightly different angles.
```

**How to use:** The connection request is the gate. Keep it humble and specific; save your pitch for after they accept.

### Prompt 20: The Post-Accept DM

```
The prospect {{prospect_name}} just accepted my LinkedIn connection request. Write a first DM.

Rules:
- Under 80 words.
- Open with something specific and genuine about them: {{specific_trigger}}.
- Give one piece of value or a relevant reference, not a pitch.
- End with a soft, low-friction question or CTA: {{cta}}.
- No fake familiarity, no "great to connect!" filler.

Give me 3 versions, each with a different opener angle.
```

**How to use:** Send within 24–48 hours of acceptance. Keep it warm, not salesy — this is the start of a relationship.

### Prompt 21: The LinkedIn Value-First DM

```
Write a LinkedIn DM to {{prospect_name}} that leads with value, not a pitch.

Open with a genuinely useful resource, insight, or observation related to their work: {{value_to_offer}}.

Then connect it to {{problem_you_solve}} naturally.

End with a soft CTA: {{cta}}.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- The value must be real and specific, not a link dump.
- Don't pitch your product in the first line.
- One ask only.
```

**How to use:** Use when you have a strong piece of content or insight. Value-first DMs convert far better than pitch-first ones.

### Prompt 22: The LinkedIn Follow-Up After No Reply

```
I sent {{prospect_name}} a LinkedIn DM {{x}} days ago and got no reply. Write a short follow-up.

Rules:
- Under 60 words.
- Acknowledge they're busy without being needy.
- Add one new piece of value or a new angle: {{new_angle}}.
- Keep it short and easy to reply to.
- No "just following up" filler.

Give me 2 options: one lighter, one more direct.
```

**How to use:** A single follow-up is fair. More than one without a reply is spam — stop after one.

### Prompt 23: The LinkedIn to Email Bridge

```
I connected with {{prospect_name}} on LinkedIn. Now I want to move the conversation to email.

Write a DM that naturally asks for their email or suggests moving to email, without being awkward or pushy.

Rules:
- Under 60 words.
- Give a reason to move to email (e.g., "easier to share this doc").
- Make it low-pressure — they can keep chatting on LinkedIn.
- Provide {{cta}}.

Give me 3 versions.
```

**How to use:** Use when you need to share attachments or the deal is getting substantive. Email is where deals actually happen.

### Prompt 24: The LinkedIn Outreach Rewrite

```
Here is a LinkedIn DM I've been using that isn't getting responses:

"""
{{current_dm}}
"""

Rewrite it using these rules:
1. Under 80 words.
2. Reference something specific about the person: {{research_notes}}.
3. Lead with value or a specific trigger, not a pitch.
4. End with one easy, low-friction ask.
5. No clichés, no "I'd love to connect," no "great to hear from you."

Output the rewrite, then list what changed and why.
```

**How to use:** Run underperforming DMs through this. It's a fast fix for generic, low-reply LinkedIn outreach.

## Follow-up & Sequence Building

### Prompt 25: The Value-Adding Follow-Up

```
Write a follow-up email to {{prospect_name}} who hasn't replied to my first email about {{topic}}.

The follow-up must ADD new value, not repeat the first email. Use one of these angles:
- A new insight: {{new_insight}}.
- A relevant resource: {{resource}}.
- A new proof point: {{new_proof}}.
- A different angle on the problem: {{new_angle}}.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- Don't ask "did you get my last email?"
- One ask.
- Reference the previous email only lightly.
```

**How to use:** Every follow-up must earn its place. If it doesn't add something new, don't send it.

### Prompt 26: The 3-Touch Sequence Builder

```
Build a 3-email follow-up sequence for {{prospect_name}} at {{company}} about {{topic}}.

Sequence requirements:
- Touch 1: trigger-based cold email, under 120 words.
- Touch 2: value-add follow-up (new insight or resource), under 100 words, sent 3-4 days later.
- Touch 3: final low-friction ask with an easy out, under 80 words, sent 3-4 days after that.

For each email, provide:
- A subject line.
- The body with {{placeholders}}.
- The CTA.
- A one-line note on why it works.

Output as a clear, copy-pasteable block.
```

**How to use:** Use this to generate a full sequence in one go, then personalize each email before sending.

### Prompt 27: The "Break the Mold" Follow-Up

```
Everyone sends "just following up" emails. I want to stand out.

Write a follow-up to {{prospect_name}} about {{topic}} that breaks the mold. The subject line and opening must be unexpected but professional — something they haven't seen 100 times this week.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- Must still be relevant and respectful.
- Must deliver a real reason to reply.
- No gimmicks, no emojis spam, no fake humor.
- The twist must serve the message, not distract from it.

Give me 3 options with different twists.
```

**How to use:** Use this when standard follow-ups have failed and you need a differentiator. Keep it professional — clever, not silly.

### Prompt 28: The Conversation-Reviver

```
A conversation with {{prospect_name}} went quiet after {{last_topic}}. Write an email that revives it.

Rules:
- Under 90 words.
- Reference the last thing you discussed: {{last_topic}}.
- Acknowledge the pause without apologizing profusely.
- Add one new, relevant thought or question that re-engages them.
- Offer an easy next step.

Give me 2 versions: one casual, one more businesslike.
```

**How to use:** Use when a thread went cold mid-conversation. The reference to the last topic makes it feel like a continuation, not a cold follow-up.

### Prompt 29: The Repositioning Follow-Up

```
I've been pitching {{prospect_name}} on {{topic}} without traction. Write a follow-up that repositions the offer.

Pick a new angle that changes the conversation:
- Frame it as solving a different problem: {{different_problem}}.
- Address a concern they might have: {{concern}}.
- Lead with a different benefit or use case: {{different_use_case}}.

Rules:
- Under 110 words.
- Must not contradict your previous messages.
- Acknowledge briefly that this is a different angle.
- One ask.
```

**How to use:** When the same pitch fails twice, don't repeat it — change the frame. This prompt forces a genuinely different offer.

### Prompt 30: The Sequence Audit

```
Here is my current follow-up sequence:

"""
{{your_sequence}}
"""

Audit it against these rules:
1. Every touch adds new value (no repeats).
2. Each email is under 120 words.
3. Each email has exactly one CTA.
4. The cadence is sensible (3-5 days apart, not daily).
5. No clichés or AI tells.
6. There's a clear final touch that gives the prospect an easy out.

For each violation, quote the offending part, explain the problem, and give a rewrite. End with a summary of the biggest fix needed.
```

**How to use:** Run your entire live sequence through this monthly. It's a quality-control pass for your whole outbound engine.

## Objection Handling

### Prompt 31: The "No Budget" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} said they don't have budget for {{your_offer}}. Write a reply.

Rules:
- Under 90 words.
- Respect the constraint; don't argue with it.
- Reframe around cost of inaction or ROI: {{roi_angle}}.
- Offer an alternative that's cheaper or deferred: {{alternative}}.
- Keep it collaborative, not pushy.

Do not discount aggressively. Offer a path, not a discount.
```

**How to use:** Budget objections are often timing objections. Offer a smaller entry or a deferred payment, and quantify the cost of waiting.

### Prompt 32: The "Not a Priority" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} said {{topic}} isn't a priority right now. Write a reply.

Rules:
- Under 90 words.
- Don't argue. Validate their priorities.
- Ask a curious question about when it might become a priority: {{timing_question}}.
- Offer to stay in touch and check back then: {{check_in_offer}}.
- Make it easy to say yes to a future touch.

Give me 2 versions: one that schedules a check-in, one that leaves the door open.
```

**How to use:** Turn "not a priority" into a scheduled future conversation. That's a win even without a deal today.

### Prompt 33: The "We Already Use X" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} says they already use {{competitor}} for {{problem_you_solve}}. Write a reply.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- Acknowledge {{competitor}} is a reasonable choice — never trash-talk it.
- Differentiate on one concrete point: {{your_differentiator}}.
- Frame that difference in terms of their goals: {{their_goal}}.
- Offer to show how the difference plays out, without forcing a switch.

Provide 2 versions: one that invites a comparison, one that asks a diagnostic question.
```

**How to use:** Respect the incumbent. Differentiation + a low-pressure "here's how we differ" beats attacking the competitor.

### Prompt 34: The "It's Too Expensive" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} says {{your_offer}} is too expensive. Write a reply.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- Don't immediately lower the price.
- Break down the value into a per-unit or outcome-based frame: {{value_breakdown}}.
- Compare to the cost of the problem: {{cost_of_problem}}.
- Offer one specific, legitimate option to reduce upfront cost if it makes sense: {{cost_option}}.

Give me 2 versions.
```

**How to use:** Price objections are value objections in disguise. Reframe the math before you ever talk discount.

### Prompt 35: The "Send Me Info" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} asked me to "send over some info." Write a reply that moves the conversation forward, not into a brochure.

Rules:
- Under 100 words.
- Thank them, but don't just dump a PDF.
- Ask one targeted question to qualify: {{qualifying_question}}.
- Offer the most relevant, specific piece of info, not everything: {{specific_info}}.
- Propose a short call to walk through it: {{cta}}.

Provide 2 versions: one that asks a question first, one that sends a focused doc + schedules a call.
```

**How to use:** "Send me info" is often a polite brush-off. Convert it into a two-way conversation, not a one-way attachment.

### Prompt 36: The "Let Me Think About It" Response

```
{{prospect_name}} said they need to think about {{your_offer}}. Write a follow-up.

Rules:
- Under 90 words.
- Give them space — no pressure.
- Offer to make the decision easier: reduce risk, add a trial, or clarify one thing: {{de_risk}}.
- Ask a single, gentle question about what's holding them back: {{holding_back_question}}.
- Set a soft timeline for a follow-up.

Give me 2 versions.
```

**How to use:** "Let me think" usually means an unspoken objection. Gently surface it with one question, and reduce the risk of the decision.

## Meeting Requests & Scheduling

### Prompt 37: The Low-Friction Meeting Request

```
Write an email to {{prospect_name}} requesting a meeting, but make the CTA extraordinarily low-friction.

Rules:
- Under 80 words.
- Don't ask "do you have time for a call?" — that's a decision they have to make.
- Instead, propose a specific time or send a calendar link with zero friction: {{calendar_link}}.
- Frame the meeting's value crisply: what they'll get in 15 minutes: {{meeting_value}}.
- Make "no" easy too — offer an email alternative.

Give me 3 versions with different scheduling approaches.
```

**How to use:** The lower the friction, the higher the booking rate. A calendar link beats a two-sided scheduling negotiation.

### Prompt 38: The "I'm Not Selling" Meeting Request

```
Write a meeting request to {{prospect_name}} that explicitly de-sells.

Rules:
- Under 80 words.
- Frame the call as informational, helpful, or exploratory — not a pitch.
- Offer something genuinely useful: a review, an audit, a walkthrough of {{topic}}: {{value_offered}}.
- No pressure, no "game-changing opportunity" hype.
- Include a low-friction CTA: {{cta}}.

Give me 3 versions.
```

**How to use:** When a prospect is wary of being sold to, lead with genuine value. This works especially well in cold outreach to busy executives.

### Prompt 39: The Meeting-Agenda Builder

```
I have a meeting booked with {{prospect_name}} from {{company}} about {{topic}}.

Build a meeting agenda that:
1. Opens with a specific, relevant observation about their situation: {{observation}}.
2. Dedicates most of the time to understanding their problem: {{questions_to_ask}}.
3. Presents a tailored recommendation, not a generic pitch.
4. Ends with clear next steps and a timeline.

Output the agenda as a short, shareable structure with time allocations (e.g., 5 min intro, 20 min discovery, 10 min recommendation, 5 min next steps).
```

**How to use:** Run this before any meeting to make sure you're prepared and the call is valuable to them, not just you.

### Prompt 40: The Post-Meeting Follow-Up

```
I just met with {{prospect_name}} at {{company}}. Write the follow-up email.

Rules:
- Within 24 hours.
- Thank them for the time.
- Recap the key points and the agreed next step: {{recap}}.
- Attach or link any promised resource: {{promised_resource}}.
- Propose the concrete next step and timeline: {{next_step}}.
- Keep it under 150 words.
```

**How to use:** Speed matters. A same-day or next-day follow-up with a clear recap and next step keeps momentum and separates you from slow competitors.

### Prompt 41: The Mutual-Action Follow-Up

```
I had a meeting with {{prospect_name}} where we both agreed to do certain things. Write a follow-up that tracks the mutual actions.

Rules:
- List what I committed to do: {{my_actions}}.
- List what they committed to do: {{their_actions}}.
- Ask a gentle question about their progress: {{progress_question}}.
- Offer help if they're stuck.
- Keep it collaborative and under 120 words.
```

**How to use:** Mutual action keeps the deal moving. This prompt turns vague promises into a tracked, accountable next step.

### Prompt 42: The "No-Show" Recovery

```
{{prospect_name}} missed a scheduled call. Write a recovery message.

Rules:
- Under 70 words.
- Assume good intent — something came up.
- Don't guilt-trip or make them feel bad.
- Offer to reschedule with a low-friction link: {{calendar_link}}.
- Keep it warm and brief.

Give me 2 versions: one that reschedules directly, one that offers to send a recap instead.
```

**How to use:** No-shows happen. A gracious, low-pressure recovery keeps the door open and often rescues the meeting.

## Deal Management & CRM Notes

### Prompt 43: The CRM Note Cleaner

```
Here is a raw, messy CRM note I just typed after a call:

"""
{{raw_crm_note}}
"""

Rewrite it into a clean, structured CRM entry with these sections:
- Company / Contact
- Stage
- Pain points / needs (as direct quotes where possible)
- Decision process & stakeholders
- Timeline / urgency
- Next steps (who does what, by when)
- Key risks / objections

Keep it factual and concise. No fluff.
```

**How to use:** Clean CRM notes keep deals honest and make handoffs easy. Run your messy notes through this after every call.

### Prompt 44: The Deal-Health Check

```
I have a deal with {{prospect_name}} at {{company}} in stage {{stage}}. Here are my notes:

"""
{{deal_notes}}
"""

Assess the deal using the MEDDIC/MEDDPICC lens or a sensible equivalent: Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion, Competition.

For each dimension, rate it Strong / Moderate / Weak and say why, then list the single most important unknown to resolve next.
```

**How to use:** A structured health check reveals where deals stall. Run it before every forecast review.

### Prompt 45: The Champion Builder

```
I want to turn {{contact_name}} at {{company}} into a champion for my deal.

Here's what I know about them: {{contact_notes}}.

Draft:
1. A message that helps them look good internally (give them ammunition to sell our solution to their team): {{ammunition}}.
2. A way to arm them with a one-pager or talking points: {{one_pager}}.
3. A question that surfaces whether they can influence the decision: {{influence_question}}.

Keep each under 100 words.
```

**How to use:** Deals close when you have a champion inside. Give them the tools to sell your solution for you.

# Bonus: The Subject-Line Matrix

Subject lines decide whether your email gets opened — which is the gate to everything else. Here's a matrix of subject-line styles, when to use each, and ready-to-adapt examples. Use it as a reference next to the subject-line prompts.

| Style | When to use | Example pattern | Why it works |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Trigger-based** | You have a specific, recent signal (funding, hire, launch) | "Congrats on the Series A" / "Noticed you're hiring for {{role}}" | Proves relevance instantly; the one style that earns the most opens |
| **Question** | You want to invite a reply, not just an open | "Is {{problem}} on your radar?" / "Quick question about {{initiative}}" | Low-effort to answer; sets up the reply |
| **Benefit** | Your offer has a clear, concrete outcome | "Cut {{metric}} by {{X}}%" / "A faster way to {{outcome}}" | States the value before they even open |
| **Curiosity gap** | You have a genuinely surprising insight to share | "The {{X}} most companies miss" / "What {{metric}} actually means" | Open loop pulls them in — but the body must deliver |
| **Low-effort reply** | Your goal is a conversation, not a meeting | "Worth a look?" / "Yes or no on {{topic}}?" | Makes replying trivial; great for cold first touch |
| **Personalized reference** | You have a real shared context (event, mutual contact, their post) | "Following up on {{event}}" / "Re: {{mutual_contact}}" | Feels like a continuation, not a cold pitch — only if the reference is real |
| **Intrigue-with-integrity** | You're targeting a skeptical, busy buyer | "The {{thing}} I'd do differently" / "Why most {{role}}s get this wrong" | Positions you as a peer with a point of view |

**How to use this matrix:**
1. Always open with a *trigger-based* or *personalized reference* subject line when you have the signal — they win on relevance.
2. When you don't have a strong trigger, fall back to a *question* or *low-effort reply* style.
3. Never use a curiosity or intrigue subject line that the email body doesn't fully deliver on. The open is only half the battle; a letdown costs you the reply.
4. A/B test two styles per send. Track opens and replies separately — an email that gets opened but not answered has a subject-line problem *or* a body problem. The matrix tells you which half to fix.

# Part 3: The Personalization Layer

The difference between a cold email that works and one that gets trashed is personalization. But "personalization" doesn't mean using the prospect's first name. It means showing you understand something *specific* about their situation.

## How to build {{research_notes}} from a prospect's site and LinkedIn

You don't need a spy tool. You need 10 focused minutes. Here's the exact workflow:

### Step 1: LinkedIn (5 minutes)
- **Their headline and "About" —** What do they highlight? What's their self-perception?
- **Their recent activity —** Posts they've shared or written. This is a goldmine of triggers.
- **Their experience —** Where did they work before? What's their career trajectory?
- **Mutual connections —** Anyone you both know. This is a warm intro path.

### Step 2: Company website (3 minutes)
- **"About" page —** Positioning, mission, size, stage.
- **Careers page —** Open roles reveal problems. Hiring a VP of Sales? They're scaling. Hiring a head of compliance? They're maturing.
- **Product/pricing page —** What do they sell, and at what price point? This tells you their customer and their margins.
- **News/updates —** Recent launches, funding, or press coverage.

### Step 3: Wider signals (2 minutes)
- **Crunchbase/YC/TheyWorkForYou** for funding and team changes.
- **Their blog or podcast appearances** — content they've made reveals what they care about.
- **Review sites (G2, Capterra, App Store)** — what customers praise and complain about reveals their gaps.

### The distillation step

Now turn raw signals into usable {{research_notes}}. The rule: **a good research note is a fact plus an implication.** A bare fact ("they hired a VP of Sales") is table stakes. A fact with an implication ("they hired a VP of Sales, so they're scaling and likely dealing with lead-gen pressure") is personalization.

## Worked example

Let's build {{research_notes}} for a fictional prospect.

**Raw signals (gathered):**
- LinkedIn: {{prospect_name}} is VP of Marketing at Acme SaaS. Previously at a high-growth startup. Posted 3 weeks ago about a new product feature launch.
- Website: Acme sells project-management software to mid-market. Careers page lists an open "Head of Customer Success" role.
- News: Acme raised a $12M Series A two months ago.

**Distilled research notes ({{research_notes}}):**
1. `Trigger:` Acme just raised a $12M Series A (2 months ago) and is hiring a Head of Customer Success — classic post-funding scaling signal.
2. `Personal detail:` {{prospect_name}} posted about the new feature launch 3 weeks ago; they're actively marketing the new direction.
3. `Implied problem:` With a Series A and a new CS hire, they're investing in retention and expansion — likely struggling with onboarding/churn as they scale.
4. `Relevant angle:` We help SaaS companies improve upgrade rates (expansion revenue) right after a funding round, when they're most motivated to show growth.

**How this changes the email:**

*Generic opening:*
> "Hi Alex, I wanted to introduce Acme and see if you're interested in improving your customer retention."

*Personalized opening built from {{research_notes}}:*
> "Hi Alex, congrats on the Series A. Between the new feature launch and hiring a Head of Customer Success, it looks like you're doubling down on expansion — a lot of post-funding SaaS teams we work with find their upgrade rate is the lever that moves the valuation most."

That's the difference. Same product, but the second email proves you did your homework — and it gives the prospect a reason to believe you understand them.

### A template for your research notes

Use this shape for every prospect:

```
TRIGGER: {{the_most_specific_recent_signal}}
PERSONAL DETAIL: {{something_specific_about_them}}
IMPLIED PROBLEM: {{what_this_likely_means_for_their_goals}}
RELEVANT ANGLE: {{how_this_connects_to_what_you_sell}}
```

Build this for each prospect before you touch a template. Ten minutes of research is the highest-ROI time you'll spend in outbound.
