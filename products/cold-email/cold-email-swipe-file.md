# Cold Email Swipe File

**120 proven cold-email templates for every situation + swipeable formulas.**

The internet is full of "top 10 cold email templates" lists, and almost all of them are dead on arrival: a greeting, a vague sentence about how you love what they're building, and a request for a 30-minute meeting. The buyer deletes it in four seconds and you never learn why.

This swipe file is built the opposite way. Every template here starts from a real situation a seller actually runs into — a lead who went quiet, a prospect who said the budget isn't approved, a customer whose renewal date is three weeks out — and gives you a short, specific email you can send today. The bodies run under 130 words, carry one single ask, and use `{{placeholders}}` so you can slot in your specifics without rewriting the logic.

Nothing here is filler. If a line doesn't earn its place in a five-second skim, it isn't in the file.

## What's inside

- 120 battle-tested cold-email templates across 12 scenarios (intro, follow-up, re-engage, referral...)
- 240 subject lines with open-rate psychology built in
- Formula breakdowns so you can write your own when you need to
- Format: PDF

## How to use this swipe file

**Template IDs (T001–T120).** Every template has a stable code — `T001`, `T002`, and so on, running consecutively from T001 to T120 across all twelve scenarios. The codes do not reset between sections, so `T047` is always `T047`. Use them like this:

- **Copy-and-send:** find the situation that matches yours, open the code block, and replace every `{{placeholder}}` with something true about your prospect or your business.
- **Keep a shared library:** paste the ID and your customized version into a spreadsheet or Notion database. When a template outperforms its neighbors, you have evidence, not a hunch.
- **A/B test by ID:** if you want to compare two openers on the same scenario, test `T006` against `T007` on the same segment and the same day. ID-level testing turns "email copy" from an art into a numbers game.

**Placeholders.** Curly braces mark the parts you must fill in:

| Placeholder | What goes in it | Common source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `{{FirstName}}` | The prospect's first name | Email signature block, LinkedIn |
| `{{Company}}` | Their company, spelled the way they spell it | Their website, LinkedIn |
| `{{Trigger}}` | The real, dated event that gave you a reason to write | Press release, job post, funding page |
| `{{SpecificPain}}` | One problem you have strong evidence exists | Their reviews, job posts, competitor teardown |
| `{{Proof}}` | A result, customer name, or number | Your case studies, benchmarks |
| `{{Offer}}` | The single thing you are offering | Your lead magnet, demo, audit |
| `{{Ask}}` | The one action you want | A reply, a link click, a 15-minute slot |
| `{{SenderFirstName}}` | Your name, if the email is sent from a person | — |

**Never send a placeholder.** An unfilled `{{Company}}` is worse than no email at all: it tells the reader you're blasting. If you can't fill a slot in ten seconds, delete that sentence instead of sending it.

**The `[ANGLE]` tag.** Each template carries a one-line note under the code block: `**When to use:** ...`. That line tells you the condition under which the email is likely to work — and, just as importantly, when it isn't. A template that needs a recent trigger but you have no trigger is a liability.

**Formula blocks.** The Formula Blocks section below defines nine named structures — PAS, BAB, 3S, SPARK, EBIN, LIST, MVF, RAP, ANCHOR and the CLOSER check — that the 120 templates are assembled from. Each template in this file is tagged with the formula it's built on, like this:

```
**Formula block:** PAS
```

The point of tagging is that you're not stuck copying. Once you see that `T034` is a BAB with a referral hook, you can rebuild the same logic for a situation that isn't in the file — which happens constantly. The formula gives you the skeleton; the placeholders give you the content.

**The subject bank (S001–S240).** The subject bank at the back is grouped by the same twelve scenarios, twenty lines each, with the psychology angle labeled for every line. Pick a subject, pick the template that matches its tone, and send. If your subject is contrarian but your body is polite, the mismatch reads as inauthentic — the pair them.

**The order of operations.** The most common mistake with a swipe file is treating it as a library to browse. Use it as a sequence:

1. Pick the scenario. Not the template — the scenario.
2. Pick the template whose "when to use" matches your actual situation.
3. Pick a subject line from the same scenario group.
4. Fill every placeholder with something verifiable.
5. Send, then log the reply in seven days.

**One ask per email.** Every template in this file contains exactly one request, because every email with two asks gets neither answered. If you need a meeting *and* a referral *and* a case study, that's three emails.

**Word count is a feature.** Long cold emails fail on mobile, where 40–60% of opens happen. If a template reads long to you, you're probably about to add a second ask. Trim it.

## Formula blocks

Nine structures cover the overwhelming majority of situations you'll hit in outbound. Learn these nine and you can write a new email without the file at all. Each block below gives you the slot structure, where it works, where it breaks, and a filled example.

### PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

**Slot structure:**

1. **Problem** — name the specific situation in one sentence, in their language.
2. **Agitate** — show what that situation costs them, concretely. Numbers, hours, missed revenue, a downstream failure. Don't editorialize.
3. **Solve** — position your offer as the thing that removes the cost, with one piece of proof.
4. **Ask** — one next step.

**When it works:** The pain is real, recent, and expensive, and you have evidence of it. Budget conversations, churn saves, missed-lead follow-ups, compliance deadlines. It's the strongest block for prospects who already know they have a problem.

**When it doesn't work:** The problem is vague, rare, or self-inflicted in a way that makes them defensive. It also collapses when there's no proof — agitate without credibility reads as exaggeration, and sophisticated buyers spot that instantly. Never agitate a problem you can't demonstrate.

**Filled example:**

> Hi {{FirstName}} — teams your size usually lose 15–20 hours a month to routing inbound leads that never get contacted, which is roughly $4,000 a month in wasted sales time at $50/hour. We cut that to under three hours by auto-enriching and scoring every form fill, and {{ClientName}} saw {{Metric}} in their first quarter. We can show you the routing logic we used — worth a look?

---

### BAB — Before, After, Bridge

**Slot structure:**

1. **Before** — paint the current state they live in, using their vocabulary.
2. **After** — describe the same workflow, solved. Make it concrete enough to picture.
3. **Bridge** — your product/service is the thing that moves them from Before to After.
4. **Ask** — one next step.

**When it works:** The change you offer is visible and describable. Process changes, tooling switches, hiring a role, adopting a platform. It works best when the "After" picture is something they'd recognize from a peer company.

**When it doesn't work:** The transformation is invisible or abstract ("unlock growth," "unify your data stack"). If you can't describe the After in a single concrete sentence, BAB will produce vague prose that your prospect will mentally translate as "no measurable outcome."

**Filled example:**

> Right now your team probably runs onboarding across a spreadsheet, three email threads, and a calendar link nobody updates. A team like yours should have a live onboarding board where the customer sees every step, the CSM sees every blocker, and nothing depends on someone remembering to send a follow-up. That's what we build — {{Company}} went from 11 days average onboarding to 6 in the first month. Want to see the board for a customer in your space?

---

### 3S — Specific, Simple, Short

**Slot structure:**

1. **Specific** — one concrete observation or detail that proves you looked.
2. **Simple** — one idea, stated once, in plain language.
3. **Short** — one ask, and a body that fits on a phone screen.

**When it works:** High-volume outbound where personalization is the differentiator but the product needs little explanation. Service businesses, agencies, freelancers, consultants, anything where a short note with one sharp detail outperforms a paragraph of positioning.

**When it doesn't work:** Complex or high-consideration purchases where the buyer needs context before engaging. It also underperforms in regulated industries where you must state credentials, scope, and compliance before asking for attention. Short beats clear, but clear still wins when the stakes are high.

**Filled example:**

> {{FirstName}} — I noticed {{Company}} is hiring two SDRs while your RevOps lead is still doing the routing manually. That's a lot of manual routing for a team scaling that fast. We set that up for {{ClientName}} in a week — fixed routing, shared inbox rules, and a weekly report they actually read. Want me to send the one-page setup?

---

### SPARK — Signal, Problem, Asset, Result, Kickoff

**Slot structure:**

1. **Signal** — the external, verifiable event that triggered the email.
2. **Problem** — what that signal usually implies for a team like theirs.
3. **Asset** — something you already have that speaks to it (a benchmark, teardown, template, short case study).
4. **Result** — the outcome the asset produced or predicts.
5. **Kickoff** — the ask.

**When it works:** You're selling into a moment — a launch, a funding round, a hiring surge, a re-org, a new market entry. The signal gives you the right to write, and the asset gives you a reason to write about *their* problem rather than your product.

**When it doesn't work:** You don't actually have a signal, or the signal is too generic ("Congrats on your growth!"). A weak signal in a SPARK email is worse than no email, because the whole email becomes a polite fiction. If you can't date the event and name it, use PAS or 3S instead.

**Filled example:**

> Hi {{FirstName}} — I saw {{Company}} opened a {{City}} office in {{Month}}, which usually means your support load is about to double before the sales team is fully in place. We benchmarked 40 SaaS companies at that stage: teams that route tier-1 tickets before headcount ramps respond to leads 3x faster in their first quarter. Here's the 2-page benchmark. Worth a look for {{FirstName}}?

---

### EBIN — Evidence, Bridge, Identify, Next

**Slot structure:**

1. **Evidence** — a result or third-party fact that makes the problem credible.
2. **Bridge** — the sentence connecting that evidence to their situation.
3. **Identify** — name the specific, named problem they have.
4. **Next** — one next step.

**When it works:** You're talking to a sophisticated buyer who discounts warm claims. Evidence-led openers work with technical buyers, enterprise procurement-minded contacts, and anyone who's been pitched by three competitors this week.

**When it doesn't work:** You have no evidence, no third-party data, and no numbers — an evidence-first email with nothing to show feels like a bluff. It also underperforms on very cold lists where the recipient has no context on you at all; there, a specific trigger warms them up better than a statistic.

**Filled example:**

> We measured support response times for 40 B2B SaaS companies and found the median time-to-first-reply is {{MedianHours}} hours, and every company above that median saw {{Pct}}% lower demo-booked rate in Q1. {{Company}}'s last two G2 reviews both mention slow first responses, which puts you right in that gap. We put together a three-step fix that doesn't need another hire. Want the steps?

---

### LIST — Lead, Insight, Solution, Test

**Slot structure:**

1. **Lead** — a short, bold opening line that stops the scroll (a claim, not a greeting).
2. **Insight** — the specific observation that proves the claim applies to them.
3. **Solution** — what you do, in one sentence.
4. **Test** — the ask, phrased as an easy-to-answer test.

**When it works:** Cold lists with no relationship, where you need to earn attention in the first line. It works best when the Lead can be a number, a mistake, or a contrarian statement. The "Test" framing ("just reply with X") is the lowest-friction ask available and converts best with busy buyers.

**When it doesn't work:** You need the recipient to trust you before engaging, and the tone comes across as presumptuous. LIST also fails when the Lead is so bold it looks like a stunt — for regulated industries, enterprise accounts, and anyone with a compliance reviewer, restraint reads better.

**Filled example:**

> Most teams lose 20% of pipeline to leads who never got a second touch.
> {{Company}} had 47 form fills in March and, by your job posts, one SDR. That gap usually costs 5–8 qualified conversations a month.
> We set up a 3-touch sequence that qualifies and books without a human writing anything.
> Reply "send it" and I'll send the 3-touch sequence for a team your size — no call needed.

---

### MVF — Mention, Value, Fit

**Slot structure:**

1. **Mention** — one specific, true thing you noticed about them.
2. **Value** — what you can do about it, stated concretely.
3. **Fit** — why that value is relevant to *their* stated priorities.

**When it works:** You're sending to a warm-ish prospect: someone who engaged with content, follows your brand, attended an event, or is one degree removed from someone who did. It converts well because the Mention reads as attention rather than targeting.

**When it doesn't work:** Your Mention is generic ("love what you're building," "big fan of your work"). Generic Mention is worse than no Mention because it signals mass outreach instantly. It also fails when you can't link their situation to your value without making a stretch claim — if you have to force the Fit, use a different block.

**Filled example:**

> {{FirstName}} — I saw your post about {{Topic}} last week, specifically the point about {{SpecificPoint}}. That's the exact failure mode we build for: teams where {{Company}}'s {{CurrentTool}} creates {{Symptom}}. The fix takes one afternoon and typically removes {{ManualHours}} hours a week. Want the one-page setup?

---

### RAP — Relevance, Advantage, Proof

**Slot structure:**

1. **Relevance** — why your message applies to this company specifically right now.
2. **Advantage** — the capability you have that alternatives don't (not a feature list — a structural difference).
3. **Proof** — one piece of evidence for that advantage.

**When it works:** Differentiated offers, technical or enterprise buyers, and competitive displacements. It works when your advantage is something a competitor structurally can't claim, not just a feature you happen to have.

**When it doesn't work:** Your product genuinely competes on the same axes as everyone else's, so "advantage" becomes marketing filler. It also requires a proof point; without one, RAP collapses into a claim and reads as exactly that. For early-stage sellers without a track record, use LIST or SPARK instead.

**Filled example:**

> {{Company}} is moving from manual spreadsheet routing to a proper workflow, which usually means picking between a heavy platform and a fragile no-code setup. We only do one thing — routing with rules that your CS team edits without us — so you get full control without a six-week implementation. {{ClientName}} switched from {{Competitor}} in nine days and their routing errors dropped {{Pct}}%. Happy to walk you through how.

---

### ANCHOR — Anchor, Name, Context, Hook

**Slot structure:**

1. **Anchor** — a shared detail that proves you're talking to the right person.
2. **Name** — name the problem in their terms.
3. **Context** — one sentence on why it matters now (a deadline, a cost, a consequence).
4. **Hook** — a reason to keep reading: a short artifact, a benchmark, a specific offer.

**When it works:** Highly researched one-to-one outreach where you've read their site, their job posts, their reviews, their funding news. ANCHOR is the most credible of the nine and it is the one that rewards the most research.

**When it doesn't work:** You don't have real detail on the company, or the anchor is so common ("we both love SaaS") that it adds nothing. ANCHOR also underperforms at volume — if you're sending it to 500 people with the same anchor, the anchor stops working and starts working against you.

**Filled example:**

> {{FirstName}} — saw your job post for a RevOps manager with "owning lead routing" in the first line. That's usually the same week {{Company}}'s inbound volume gets hard to keep up with, and it's the moment routing rules break. Right now leads probably fall into one of six spreadsheets. I put together a teardown of your current funnel with {{N}} fixes — want me to send it over?

---

### The CLOSER check — Confirm, Link, Offer, Signal, Expect

This isn't an opening formula; it's the five-line check you run on every email before you hit send. If any slot is empty, the email isn't ready.

1. **Confirm** — the first line proves you know who they are. It should be impossible to send the same line to 500 people.
2. **Link** — the second line connects that detail to a problem they plausibly have.
3. **Offer** — you give something of value before asking for anything (a benchmark, a teardown, a short answer, a credit).
4. **Signal** — the ask is a single, low-friction next step with a clear reason.
5. **Expect** — you tell them what you'll do next ("if I don't hear back by Friday, I'll close the loop"), which makes silence comfortable and increases replies.

Run the check on all 120 templates. They all pass it. Your rewritten versions should too.

## 1) Cold First Touch

Ten openers for the very first email — the one that decides whether a conversation happens. Every one of them is anchored on something verifiable.

### T001 — Funding-triggered opener

**Formula block:** SPARK

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — congrats on the {{RoundStage}} round. When {{Company}} adds the {{NextHireCount}} people on your jobs page, inbound usually gets messy before the team catches up.

We help SaaS companies at that stage auto-route and qualify inbound so nobody falls through.

We put together a 2-page benchmark of what 30 companies at your ARR band saw in their first quarter after raising. Worth sending?
```

**When to use:** Use within two weeks of a funding announcement, and only if you can name a concrete job listing or hiring signal.

### T002 — Hiring-post opener

**Formula block:** ANCHOR

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I saw {{Company}} posted for {{JobTitle}} with {{PainPhrase}} in the requirements.

That role usually exists because the current process needs a person glued to it, and that person becomes the bottleneck by month three.

We build the automation layer that removes that glue for {{Industry}} teams, so the hire becomes a multiplier instead of a patch.

Want the two-minute teardown of how {{SimilarCompany}} handled the same transition?
```

**When to use:** Use when a job post mentions a manual, repetitive, or scaling process you can automate.

### T003 — Product-launch opener

**Formula block:** SPARK

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — saw {{Company}} launched {{ProductName}} this week.

Launches usually create a second problem: the sales team now has two things to explain and one playbook that covers both.

We built a one-page conversation guide for {{Company}}'s new {{ProductName}} that tells reps what to say, what not to say, and which objections show up first. It takes ten minutes to review.

Send it over?
```

**When to use:** Use within a week of a product or feature launch, ideally while launch momentum is still visible.

### T004 — Competitor-move opener

**Formula block:** RAP

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — noticed {{Competitor}} launched {{CompetitorFeature}} yesterday, and it looks aimed squarely at {{Company}}'s {{Segment}}.

They've got distribution. You have {{SpecificAdvantage}} — {{Proof}} and {{ProofTwo}}.

I mapped what their launch changes for a team your size, and what it doesn't touch at all. Want the map?
```

**When to use:** Use when a competitor's launch genuinely threatens one specific segment of the prospect's business.

### T005 — Review-based opener

**Formula block:** EBIN

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I read {{Company}}'s three most recent reviews on {{ReviewSite}}. The word {{RepeatedWord}} showed up in all three, and in two of them it was attached to {{SpecificIssue}}.

We've got a fix that takes {{Timeframe}} and doesn't require a new hire. Want the three steps?
```

**When to use:** Use when 2+ reviews name the same problem, and you have a credible benchmark for that problem.

### T006 — The direct-solution opener

**Formula block:** 3S

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — do you still handle {{ManualTask}} manually?

Most {{JobTitle}}s in {{Industry}} do until it breaks. We automate it for {{Outcome}}, and {{ClientName}} cut {{Metric}} in {{Timeframe}}.

If you're already looking at this, I can send the {{Deliverable}} — no call needed.
```

**When to use:** Use when the prospect's job title strongly implies the manual task exists; keep it to one clear question.

### T007 — The benchmark-offer opener

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Quick question about {{Company}}'s {{ProcessArea}}.

We pulled {{Number}} data points on {{ProcessArea}} for {{Industry}} companies. {{HeadlineStat}}.

The three companies that beat it all did the same three things. I can send the one-pager — want it?
```

**When to use:** Use when you own proprietary data or a study that maps directly to one of the prospect's processes.

### T008 — The event-attendee opener

**Formula block:** MVF

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — saw you were at {{EventName}} in {{Month}}, and specifically that you joined the {{SessionName}} session.

Most people who sit in that session are wrestling with {{SpecificPain}} right now, usually because {{RootCause}}.

We work on that exact problem. I put together a short write-up on {{Topic}} from that session's angle — send it?
```

**When to use:** Use when the event program is public and you can confirm the specific session attended.

### T009 — The hiring-spike opener

**Formula block:** SPARK

```

Rapid hiring usually means the operating processes haven't caught up yet, and {{Title}} is the one who feels it first.

Want it?
```

**When to use:** Use when the job count has clearly grown and you can point to a process that scales badly with headcount.

### T010 — The mutual-connection opener

**Formula block:** PAS

```

**When to use:** Use when you have a genuine referral who gave you a real detail; never fabricate the referral.

## 2) Follow-Up Without Reply

Follow-ups are where most sequences die — too soft, too repetitive, or too needy. These ten add new information each time instead of asking again.

### T011 — Add-the-new-data follow-up

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — circling back with something new rather than asking again.

We just published {{ResourceTitle}}, which includes {{RelevantStat}} for {{Industry}} companies.

If {{SpecificProblem}} is still on your list, that's the section worth reading: {{Section}}.

Here: {{Link}}
```

**When to use:** Use as follow-up 2 when you have genuinely new data or a new resource since the first email.

### T012 — The one-line-check-in

**Formula block:** 3S

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — just checking whether this landed in a busy week.

If it's not a priority right now, say the word and I'll stop following up.

If it is, reply with {{ReplyWord}} and I'll send {{SpecificThing}} directly.
```

**When to use:** Use as follow-up 3, after two sends with no reply; make the "stop following up" offer real.

### T013 — The deadline follow-up

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I noticed {{Company}}'s {{Event}} is on {{Date}}.

If {{Problem}} is still slowing that down, we can get it handled before then. {{ClientName}} did it in {{Timeframe}}.

Should I send the {{Deliverable}}, or is this not a priority right now?
```

**When to use:** Use when a real, dated event (conference, launch, deadline, hiring close) creates natural timing pressure.

### T014 — The permission-to-close follow-up

**Formula block:** ANCHOR

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — last note from me on {{Topic}}.

I've got a couple of ideas for {{Company}}'s {{SpecificChallenge}}, and I'd rather send them than keep filling your inbox.

Want the {{Deliverable}}, or should I close this out?

Either answer is fine — just reply with one word.
```

**When to use:** Use as the final follow-up before going dormant; it lowers the cost of a reply to one word.

### T015 — The new-case-study follow-up

**Formula block:** RAP

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — since I last wrote, {{ClientName}} shipped the exact thing we discussed. Results: {{MetricOne}}, {{MetricTwo}}.

The relevant part for {{Company}} is {{SpecificRelevance}} — it took {{Timeframe}} with {{TeamSize}} people.

Case study attached. Worth ten minutes of your time?
```

**When to use:** Use when a new customer story maps closely to the prospect's situation and arrived after your first email.

### T016 — The shift-in-their-world follow-up

**Formula block:** SPARK

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — something changed on your side: {{TriggerDescription}}.

That usually means {{Implication}} — which is exactly the gap we close for {{Industry}} teams.

I've attached the short version, since it now matters more than it did in {{Month}}. Want to take a look?
```

**When to use:** Use when the prospect has posted news about a change that increases the urgency of your offer.

### T017 — The two-option follow-up

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — to make this easy, two options:

1. I send the {{Deliverable}} and you skim it when you have ten minutes.
2. I send a {{LengthMinute}}-minute walkthrough that shows the setup end to end.

Reply "1" or "2". If neither, I'll leave you alone.
```

**When to use:** Use when the buyer hasn't engaged and you want to shrink the decision to a single keystroke.

### T018 — The relevant-obstacle follow-up

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I suspect one of three things is true:

1. This isn't a priority right now.
2. You disagree with my read of {{SpecificProblem}}.
3. I picked the wrong person.

If it's 2 or 3, reply with a sentence and I'll fix my notes. If it's 1, no problem — I'll check back in {{Months}}.

Which is it?
```

**When to use:** Use when you've made a concrete claim that could be wrong; it invites correction, which is easier than a yes.

### T019 — The social-proof follow-up

**Formula block:** EBIN

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — a quick data point since my last email.

We published {{StudyName}} this month: {{Number}} {{Industry}} companies, {{HeadlineStat}}.

{{Company}} showed up in the data as {{RelevantObservation}}. The section that matched your situation is about {{Topic}}.

Here's the link: {{Link}}. No call required.
```

**When to use:** Use when new data includes the prospect's segment or company, and they were on a list you could plausibly have.

### T020 — The graceful-fourth follow-up

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I've reached out {{Number}} times about {{Topic}}, so this is the last one.

If {{Problem}} becomes a problem for {{Company}}, here's what I'd do: {{OneLineSolution}}.

No reply needed. But if you'd like the {{Deliverable}}, reply "send it" and I'll send it straight away.
```

**When to use:** Use as a self-aware final touch; keep the tone factual rather than apologetic.

## 3) Re-Engagement / Dormant Lead

These pick up threads that died — six months, two years, or longer. The job is to acknowledge the gap honestly and give a new reason to write.

### T021 — The long-dormant check-in

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — it's been {{TimeSince}}, which is long enough that I should probably start with "why now."

{{TriggerDescription}} made {{SpecificProblem}} much more expensive for {{Industry}} companies than it was then.

Same fix applies, different context. Worth 15 minutes, or too much on your plate?
```

**When to use:** Use after six months or more of silence, and only when you have a real new reason.

### T022 — The new-trigger re-engagement

**Formula block:** SPARK

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — we spoke in {{Month}} about {{PastTopic}}. Since then: {{Company}} has {{NewDevelopment}}, which changes the math.

Three of our customers at the same stage saw {{Metric}} in the first quarter after {{NewDevelopment}}.

Can I show you what that looked like for a team your size?

**When to use:** Use when you have a genuine past conversation and a concrete development since then.

### T025 — The new-person re-engagement

## 9) Inbound Lead Follow-Up

```

**When to use:** Use immediately after a download, so the lead knows the content was worth requesting.

### T082 — The demo-request response

**Formula block:** 3S

```

**When to use:** Use when an inbound lead asks for pricing; give the real number early and qualify on usage.

**When to use:** Use when the lead explicitly requested a call; make one genuine attempt and offer a written alternative.

## 10) Post-Sale or Renewal

The customer already bought. These emails protect revenue, surface expansion, and start renewals on time rather than in a panic.

### T091 — The 30-day value check

**Formula block:** EBIN

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — you're about a month into {{ProductName}}.

Two quick questions:
1. Has {{TeamName}} hit {{TargetOutcome}} yet?
2. What's the one thing still annoying you about it?

If (2) has an answer, I can usually fix it within a week. Reply honestly — it's the fastest way to make this worth renewing.
```

**When to use:** Use 30 days after go-live, before the customer has formed a habit of ignoring you.

### T092 — The renewal 90-days-out opener

**Formula block:** ANCHOR

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — {{Company}}'s renewal is {{Date}}, which is about {{Timeframe}} away.

Two things I'd like to do first:
1. Review what worked and what didn't over the last {{ReviewPeriod}}.
2. Flag anything {{Company}} is using that you'd want to drop before renewal.

Reply "review" and I'll send the prep sheet. It takes about {{Timeframe}} and it makes the renewal conversation short.
```

**When to use:** Use at least 90 days before renewal; the goal is a short renewal conversation, not a long one.

### T093 — The usage-dropoff check-in

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — I noticed {{Company}}'s usage of {{FeatureName}} dropped from {{PriorUsage}} to {{CurrentUsage}} over the last {{Timeframe}}.

That usually means one of three things:
1. The workflow moved and we haven't been told.
2. Something broke and nobody said anything.
3. It solved the problem and you moved on.

Which is it? If it's 2, I want to fix it today.
```

**When to use:** Use when usage data shows a real decline; the three options make it easy to reveal a churn signal.

### T094 — The expansion conversation

**Formula block:** BAB

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — {{Company}} is now doing {{NewUseCase}} that {{CustomerName}} wasn't doing a year ago.

Two options:
1. Keep everything as-is and use {{AdjacentProduct}} manually on the side.
2. Add {{AdjacentProduct}} to your plan for {{AdditionalPrice}}.

Option 1 usually costs about {{HiddenCost}} a month in staff time. Want me to show you the side-by-side?
```

**When to use:** Use when the customer's usage has expanded into a natural adjacent need; price the option honestly.

### T095 — The renewal-risk save

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — before {{RenewalDate}}, I want to check one thing honestly.

Is there anything about {{ProductName}} that you've stopped using, or that you'd drop if you could? Renewal should be because it's still valuable, not because a date came up.

Tell me what's not working — I'll either fix it or tell you to leave. Either answer saves you money.
```

**When to use:** Use when you suspect a quiet dissatisfaction; it's much cheaper to hear the truth now.

### T096 — The multi-year upgrade pitch

**Formula block:** RAP

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — {{Company}} is on {{CurrentPlan}}, and you're getting close to {{LimitThreshold}} on {{LimitFeature}}.

The {{UpgradedPlan}} includes {{UpgradedFeature}}, which removes that ceiling and adds {{OtherFeature}}.

For {{ClientName}}, moving up at that point was {{DeltaCost}} a month and saved {{HoursSaved}} hours a week in {{TeamName}}.

Worth comparing before renewal, or does {{CurrentPlan}} still fit?
```

**When to use:** Use when a plan limit is approaching; frame the upgrade as removing friction, not as spending more.

### T097 — The advocacy request

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — {{CustomerName}} achieved {{Metric}}, and you were the one who made that happen.

Would you be open to:
1. A 30-minute reference call with a peer company, or
2. A short written quote with your name and title?

No pressure, and nothing gets published without your sign-off. Either way, thank you — the {{Metric}} result was the reason we built {{FeatureName}} in the first place.
```

**When to use:** Use after a clear win with a named champion; make each option small and low-risk.

### T098 — The testimonial-to-case-study upgrade

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — thank you for the kind words about {{ProductName}}.

Would you be open to turning it into a short case study? About {{Timeframe}} of your time, and we'd cover {{Details}} — the numbers, the before/after, and what made it work.

You'd review and approve every line before anything goes out. Worth a yes or no?
```

**When to use:** Use right after a warm compliment; ask for the smallest version of the ask so the answer is easy.

### T099 — The onboarding-derailment rescue

**Formula block:** 3S

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — {{CustomerName}} signed up {{DaysAgo}} days ago and hasn't finished {{OnboardingStep}}.

That's usually one of two things:
1. The setup takes longer than expected.
2. The value only lands once someone else is involved.

If it's 1, I can finish it for you in a 20-minute call. If it's 2, who else should I loop in?

**When to use:** Use when onboarding stalls early; the two-cause diagnosis avoids a long generic check-in list.

### T100 — The post-renewal reset

**Formula block:** BAB

```
Hi {{CustomerFirstName}} — you're renewed through {{RenewalDate}}. Rather than leave it there, let's make the next year better.

Before: {{CurrentUsagePattern}}.
After: {{TargetUsagePattern}} — we did this for {{SimilarCustomer}} using {{Mechanism}} and it took {{Timeframe}}.

I'll set up {{SessionType}} in {{Month}}. Worth it?

**When to use:** Use immediately after a renewal so the account doesn't drift into passive mode.

## 12) Break-Up Email

The break-up email is the highest-performing email most people never send. These are short, dignified, and genuinely final — with one exception: a real re-opening condition.

### T111 — The clean break-up

**Formula block:** 3S

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I haven't heard back, so I'll assume this isn't a priority for {{Company}}.

I'll stop here. If it becomes one later, reply to this email and it'll come to me — no follow-ups, no sequence.

Either way, thanks for your time.
```

**When to use:** Use as the final email after 4–6 touches with no response; truly close the loop.

### T112 — The break-up with a parting gift

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — no reply, so this is my last email on {{Topic}}.

Before I go: here's {{ResourceName}}, which covers {{SpecificProblem}} on its own. It's useful whether you buy from us or not.

Take it, and if {{Problem}} ever becomes urgent, reply and it'll come straight to me.

Thanks, {{SenderFirstName}}.
```

**When to use:** Use when you have a genuinely valuable standalone resource; deliver value with no expectation attached.

### T113 — The break-up with a trigger

**Formula block:** SPARK

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I'll stop emailing now.

One exception, and it's a real one: if {{TriggerEvent}} happens, that's usually when {{Problem}} gets expensive, and I'll send you {{Resource}} then.

Otherwise, you won't hear from me again. Thanks for reading.
```

**When to use:** Use when there is a genuine future trigger; state it so the exception feels like a rule, not a promise.

### T114 — The break-up with a specific door

**Formula block:** ANCHOR

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — closing the loop on {{Topic}}.

If the reason this stalled is {{ReasonOne}}, or {{ReasonTwo}}, or {{ReasonThree}} — tell me which, and I'll do the sensible thing:

1. Wrong timing → I'll follow up in {{Month}}.
2. Not a fit → I'll stop for good.
3. Wrong person → I'll ask you for one name and then leave you alone.

If I don't hear back, that's answer enough. Thanks, {{FirstName}}.
```

**When to use:** Use when you suspect a fixable cause; make each reply path require minimal effort.

### T115 — The respectful-goodbye

**Formula block:** PAS

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — I know outreach is noise, and I don't want to be one more email in your inbox.

I'm going to close this out. If you're ever dealing with {{Problem}} and want a second opinion, my reply address works even after I stop emailing.

Thanks for your time, and good luck with {{Company}}.
```

**When to use:** Use for a warm but non-responsive contact; leave a low-pressure door open without expectations.

### T116 — The break-up that asks for a diagnosis

**Formula block:** EBIN

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — before I drop this, one question that would genuinely help.

Out of {{Number}} similar emails last quarter, the most common reply was "{{CommonReply}}." Is that you, or is something else going on?

Either way, thank you — it's useful information and I won't follow up either way.
```

**When to use:** Use when you have real reply data; the diagnostic question can be answered with one line and teaches you something.

### T117 — The break-up with a calendar offer

**Formula block:** LIST

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — closing out this thread.

If it'd help to have a second opinion on {{Problem}} without any commitment, I've got {{Timeframe}} open on {{Date}}.

Or don't reply and I'll assume we're done. Either way, thanks for the time.
```

**When to use:** Use when a short, low-stakes conversation genuinely might solve something; make silence explicitly acceptable.

### T118 — The break-up to a specific role change

**Formula block:** MVF

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — since you moved into {{NewRole}} at {{Company}}, my note about {{OldTopic}} probably landed at the wrong desk.

I'm closing this one out. If {{Problem}} ends up on your plate, {{Solution}} is what we do — reply and I'll send the details.

Otherwise, good luck with the new role.
```

**When to use:** Use when you know the contact changed roles; acknowledge it and let the old thread close cleanly.

### T119 — The break-up after a good conversation

**Formula block:** ANCHOR

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — thanks for the time on {{CallDate}}. The part about {{SpecificDetail}} you shared was genuinely useful to me.

I'm going to stop chasing this. If {{Problem}} becomes a priority, reply and you'll get a straight answer and a useful artifact — no pitch.

Thanks again, {{FirstName}}.
```

**When to use:** Use after a warm but non-converting conversation; the relationship is worth more than the deal.

### T120 — The break-up with a future door

**Formula block:** 3S

```
Hi {{FirstName}} — last email, promise.

If {{Company}} hits {{TriggerEvent}} — {{SpecificEvent}} — I'll check in once. If it's not relevant then, that's the end of it.

No response needed now. Just wanted you to know there's no hard feelings and one door open.

Thanks, {{SenderFirstName}}.
```

**When to use:** Use as the final touch in a sequence; keep it short, warm, and genuinely one-email-only.

## Personalization checklist

Before you send anything, spend ten minutes on this. Every row is a place to find a fact that makes your email specific — and specificity is the single best predictor of a reply.

| What to research | Where to find it | What it replaces in your template | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The prospect's exact job title and tenure | LinkedIn profile headline + experience | `{{FirstName}}` context, role-specific phrasing | Length at a company changes what they care about |
| A public trigger: funding, hire, launch, office, event | Press, LinkedIn activity, company news | The `[TRIGGER]` slot in SPARK/ANCHOR templates | An event causes the moment to be now |
| The company's current tooling (from their stack) | Job posts, careers page, tech pages, G2 | `{{Tool}}`, "your current stack" phrasing | Naming the real tool makes you credible |
| The specific problem their reviews name | G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, app-store reviews | `{{Problem}}`, `[PAIN]` slots | Reviews are what customers already felt |
| Who owns the problem (the decision-maker) | LinkedIn title, company org chart, job posts | `{{Role}}`, `{{DecisionMaker}}` | Wrong person = wasted email |
| Whether they have competitors you can reference | Your own deal notes, public comparisons | `{{Competitor}}` | A named competitor is proof you looked |
| The "how much it costs" numbers (their side) | Their headcount, pricing, their reviews | `{{Metric}}`, `{{Hours}}` | Agitation without a number is just pleading |
| A company size/industry match from your own wins | Your CRM, case studies | `{{SimilarCompany}}`, `{{Proof}}` | Similarity is what makes proof transfer |
| The proposal's seniority / decision power | LinkedIn, org chart, their content | `{{Role}}` framing | Match their level of authority |
| Their recent activity (posts, comments, shares) | LinkedIn activity, their company blog | `{{Topic}}`, `{{Insight}}` | A fresh signal beats a static one |
| The budget/planning cycle if public | Job posts, announcements, industry norms | The before at in scenario 6 and 5 | Timing is a better objection than price |
| Whether you have a mutual connection | LinkedIn "mutual connections", your CRM | `{{MutualName}}` | Warmth multiplies reply rate |
| The pain in *their* words (quote them) | Their posts, support pages, their language | `{{SpecificPain}}` | Your words don't persuade them; theirs do |

**Order of operations:** research first, then pick the template second, then write third. If you pick a template first, you'll be tempted to reverse-engineer a reason to use it. The template is the last decision.

## FAQ

**The email doesn't get replies. What am I doing wrong?**
Most likely your placeholders weren't filled with something the prospect recognizes as true of them. A `{{Trigger}}` that isn't a real event reads as an excuse to write. Re-check the checklist: if you can't finish it in ten minutes for a given prospect, that prospect shouldn't get a personalized pitch. Secondly, check your subject line. If your subject is a question but your body is a claim, the mismatch kills trust. Match tone.

**Should I send follow-ups to people who never opened?**
No. If they never opened, your first line never got read — so your follow-up is the second chance. The exception is a genuinely new trigger that would make *anyone's* first line more relevant. Sending the same subject three times is worse than sending a new one once.

**How do I keep the cadence from feeling aggressive?**
Space them out and make each one add new value. Standard rhythm: touch 1 on day 0, touch 2 on day 4–6, touch 3 around day 10–14, then the break-up at day 21–28. The break-up emails are what make you look professional — they tell the buyer when to stop hearing from you. Silence is not anxiety if you've said you'll stop.

**What if I'm a freelancer and not a "company"?**
Use your own name instead of `{{Company}}`. The templates work the same. The placeholders that change: `{{SenderFirstName}}` becomes the whole sender identity, and `{{Proof}}` becomes one specific project rather than a customer list. Short, specific, one ask — that's the formula regardless of scale.

**Should I personalize the subject line too, or keep it clean?**
Both. A personalized subject (a name, a company, a real number) raises opens when it's true. But don't put their name in the subject *and* in the first line — that reads as spam. Use a personalized subject OR a personalized opening, not both on the same email.

**The template is 90 words but my offer is complex. What do I do?**
Choose a different scenario. Complex offers that need explanation are a "For a Demo or Call" problem, not a cold-first-touch problem. The first email should be gate, not a brochure. You get a second email only if you earn it — which means you can't cram the whole offer into the first one.

**When should I send?**
Tuesday–Thursday, 8–11 AM in the buyer's timezone is the median. But median isn't your answer: test. If your list is in a different timezone than you, schedule for their morning, not the back. If you see an open rate below ~30% or a reply rate below ~10%, the issue is usually the subject line and the opening line — fix those before the body.

**Can I put all twelve scenarios in sequence for the same prospect?**
You can, but you shouldn't. No buyer needs all twelve. The sequence that works for a warm inbound lead (scenario 9) is not the sequence for a cold one (scenario 1). Pick the scenario that matches reality, and only escalate to a break-up (scenario 12) when you've genuinely exhausted the other angles.

**What does a "reply-required" subject mean for my campaign?**
It means the email is designed to be answered by a single keystroke — "send it," "1," "not now." That reduces the cognitive cost of replying. Use those subjects only when the body asks exactly one thing that has a one-word answer. If your body asks for a meeting time, a reply-required subject is a bait-and-switch.
