# Student Study AI Prompt Pack

**Summarize, quiz and memorize faster with 40+ study prompts.**

This pack turns your AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, or any LLM) into a dedicated study partner. Instead of asking vague things like *"help me study"* (which earns you generic, useless replies), you'll paste a specific, proven prompt. Each prompt uses a real learning technique — active recall, spaced repetition, the Feynman method, elaborative interrogation, interleaving, and more — so you're not just getting faster, you're learning *how to learn* better.

There are 50 prompts here, organized by purpose: summarizing, flashcards, quizzes, essays, exam prep, memory, planning, and tackling hard concepts. Eight fill-in-the-blank templates give you structured frameworks (Cornell notes, spaced repetition plans, revision grids, error logs). Four worked "subject packs" show you exactly how the same prompt plays out for science, humanities, maths, and business. And a 30-day sprint table ties it all together into a plan you can start tonight.

This works with any subject. Paste your textbook chapter, your lecture notes, a slide deck, or a PDF you've pasted into the chat. You don't need any special setup — just an account with any modern AI chatbot.

## How to Use This Pack

**Step 1 — Copy a prompt.** Every prompt is inside a fenced code block. Click the copy button on the block (or select the text and copy it). Nothing else in this file gets copied with it.

**Step 2 — Fill in the `{{placeholders}}`.** These are the parts in double curly braces. Replace them with your real content. The most important one is `{{your material}}` — paste your textbook chapter, class notes, or slide text right there. If your material is long, you can paste it in a separate message first and tell the AI "the notes I just pasted" instead of re-pasting.

**Step 3 — Paste and press send.** The AI works through the prompt. Most prompts are written to ask for one clean, structured output. If it ignores a part, just reply: *"You missed the {{X}} part — please include it."*

**Step 4 — Follow up.** The prompts are starting points, not scripts. You can always reply with *"Make it harder,"* *"Give me 10 more practice questions,"* or *"Explain that section again but simpler."* The prompt pack's power is that it gets you a *good first output* every time; your follow-ups make it great.

**Four habits that make this pack work:**

1. **Always use the technique, not just the output.** Every prompt names its technique (for example, *retrieval practice* or *spaced repetition*). Mild the technique — it's the part that makes the studying actually stick, not just the writing.
2. **Do the thinking yourself.** The AI produces questions, outlines, and summaries. *You* do the recalling. Never read a generated flashcard out loud and call it studying — cover the answer and retrieve it from memory first.
3. **Revisit prompts across days.** Spaced repetition only works if you come back. The 30-day sprint in the last section schedules this for you.
4. **Keep your answers in a note file.** Start a running doc called "Study log" and paste your best outputs there (Cornell notes, revision grids, error logs). By exam time you'll have a complete review file you built with the AI's help.

### Power follow-up commands

A prompt isn't a one-shot spell — it's the opening move of a conversation. These one-liner replies turn any output into a deeper study session. Keep them handy:

- *"Explain the hardest part of that again, but use a different analogy."*
- *"Turn the 3 most important points into flashcards."*
- *"Ask me the questions one at a time and wait for my answer."*
- *"Give me a harder version of question 4."*
- *"Make it shorter — 5 bullets maximum."*
- *"Now quiz me on the parts I got wrong."*
- *"What's the most common mistake students make here?"*
- *"Connect this to what I learned about {{related topic}}."*
- *"Show me the reasoning step by step, not just the answer."*
- *"Turn this outline into a timed-exam plan."*
- *"What would a top-scoring exam answer include that mine doesn't?"*
- *"Give me 3 more practice questions on this."*
- *"Summarize our whole conversation so far as a one-page study sheet."*
- *"If I only had 10 minutes to review this, what should I look at?"*

### The technique quick-reference

Every prompt in this pack is built on a real, evidence-backed technique. This table tells you which technique does what and where to find it. Learn the names — you'll recognize them in your own future studying, and you'll be able to tell the AI exactly which * you want.

| Technique | What it's for | Prompts that use it |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| Cornell method | Structured notes you can self-quiz from | 1, Template 1 |
| Feynman technique | Checking true understanding by plain-language explanation | 2, Template 4 |
| Retrieval practice | Strengthening memory by pulling info OUT, not just re-reading | 9, 10, 12, 13, 21 |
| Spaced repetition | Scheduling reviews at growing intervals to beat the forgetting curve | 34, Template 2 |
| Interleaving | Mixing topics to build exam-ready flexible recall | 14 |
| Elaborative interrogation | Asking "why is this true?" to deeply encode facts | Template 5 |
| Dual coding | Combining words + images/diagrams for two retrieval paths | 7, Template 6 |
| Chunking | Grouping info into 3-7 item units that fit working memory | 36 |
| Method of loci (memory palace) | Memorizing ordered lists by placing vivid images in a familiar space | 35 |
| Peg-word system | Memorizing numbered lists using number-image pegs | 37 |
| Worked examples | Learning from solved problems + your own attempts | 49 |
| Socratic questioning | Guided self-discovery via questions, not lectures | 50 |
| Pomodoro | Focused 25-min blocks with breaks for sustainable study | 42, 40 |
| Error analysis | Diagnosing *why* you miss questions to stop repeating mistakes | 29, Template 7 |

But don't take the table's word for it — here's the short version of *why* these techniques beat plain re-reading, so you trust the method enough to stick with it:

- **Re-reading and highlighting feel productive but build almost nothing.** Psychologists call this the *fluency illusion*: the text looks familiar, so your brain mistakes familiarity for knowledge. Every technique in this pack replaces passive re-reading with an act of *retrieval* — pulling information out of memory — which is the single strongest proven lever for long-term retention.
- **Forgetting is predictable, so you can schedule against it.** The **forgetting curve** drops steeply within 24 hours and then keeps falling. Spaced repetition (Template 2, Prompt 34) simply reviews a topic right before it would fade — which is when a small effort buys the most memory. This is why five 10-minute reviews across a month beat one 50-minute cram.
- **Interleaving makes retrieval harder, and that's the point.** Mixing topics (Prompt 14) feels harder than studying one subject at a time — but that very difficulty strengthens the memory and better mirrors how exams actually mix questions. Desirable difficulty is a real effect: if studying feels too easy, you're probably not learning much.
- **Explaining is the ultimate comprehension test.** The Feynman method (Template 4) and elaborative interrogation (Template 5) force you to notice the gap between "I've seen this" and "I can produce this." That gap is exactly where real understanding grows.
- **Your working memory holds only ~4–7 items.** Chunking (Prompt 36) and memory palaces (Prompt 35) work *with* that limit instead of against it, compressing many items into few, unforgettable units.

The prompts aren't magic — the techniques are. The AI just makes the techniques effortless to run, which is the part that usually kills good intentions.

## Template 1 — Cornell Notes

The Cornell method splits your page into three zones: a narrow left column for cues/questions, a wide right column for the main notes, and a bottom strip for a summary. The left column is where *active recall* happens later — you cover the right side and quiz yourself using only the cues.

**When to use it:** During lectures or while reading a chapter. It forces you to capture the material in a way you can quiz yourself from tomorrow.

```
CORNEL NOTES — [Subject] — [Topic] — [Date]

┌──────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ CUES (questions &       │ NOTES (main content)                   │
│ keywords — fill AFTER,  │                                       │
│ during review)          │                                       │
├──────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Q: What is the main     │ • Key point 1: ...                     │
│   argument?             │ • Supporting detail: ...               │
│ Q: Define term X.       │ • Example: ...                         │
│ Keyword: [term]         │ • Diagram/equation: ...                 │
│ Q: How does X connect   │ • ...                                  │
│   to Y?                 │                                       │
├──────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ SUMMARY (one paragraph, your own words):                         │
│ In this topic, the key idea is ... The most important            │
│ distinction is ... I still don't understand ...                  │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**How to use it:** During class, fill only the NOTES column. Within 24 hours, fill the CUES column (turn each chunk of notes into a question). Then cover the notes side and answer the cues out loud. Finish with the SUMMARY — if you can't write it, you didn't understand yet. A common mistake is filling the cue column *during* class instead of after — cues exist for later self-quizzing, not for live capture. And never skip the summary: if you can't write it, you haven't understood it yet, and that's exactly what you need to know before moving on.

## Template 2 — Spaced Repetition Plan

This is your master schedule for *when* to review each topic. The science is simple: you remember more if you review at growing intervals — roughly 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days — than if you cram once. You build this with prompt #34 (the spaced repetition scheduler) and update it every day.

```
SPACED REPETITION PLAN — [Course/Subject]

Topic          | First        | Review 1    | Review 2    | Review 3    | Next
               | learned (d0) | (+1 day)    | (+3 days)   | (+7 days)   | (+21d)
---------------+--------------+-------------+-------------+-------------+------
[Topic A]      | [date]       | [date]      | [date]      | [date]      | [date]
[Topic B]      | ...          | ...         | ...         | ...         | ...
[Topic C]      | ...          | ...         | ...         | ...         | ...

Review method each session: 1) retrieve from memory (no notes),
2) check answers, 3) re-learn what you missed, 4) mark confidence.
Confidence: ● strong / ◐ shaky / ○ forgot → re-schedule forgotten
items to tomorrow, don't just move them out. The two mistakes that wreck spaced repetition: (1) cheating by reading the notes *before* trying to recall, and (2) reviewing a topic you already nailed instead of the one you forgot. Trust the schedule — the topic you want to skip is usually the one that needs the review most.
```

**How to use it:** Every time you learn a topic, add a row. Every study session, open this table first and handle anything due today. Rules: (1) always try to recall *before* you look at notes; (2) if you rate yourself "forgot," that topic comes back tomorrow, not in 3 days.

## Template 3 — Revision Grid

A revision grid is a one-page map of the whole exam syllabus. You list every topic, rate how well you know it, and track your coverage. It turns "I don't know where to start" into a visible, clickable plan.

```
REVISION GRID — [Exam / Course]

State         | Topics covered              | Confidence | Notes / gaps
              |                             | (1-5)     |
--------------+-----------------------------+-----------+-----------------
[Chapter 1]   | [list subtopics]            | 3         | Need to redo
              |                             |           | practice Qs on X
[Chapter 2]   | ...                         | 2         | ...
--------------+-----------------------------+-----------+-----------------
Total coverage: [N] of [M] topics. Weakest 3: [list].
Next session priority: [weakest topic with biggest marks weight].
```

**How to use it:** Fill it in after your first pass through the material (prompt #43 generates a draft of the topic list for you). Update confidence after every review. Always work the *lowest-confidence, highest-marks* topic first — that's the highest-value studying you can do. Refresh the grid after every study session, not just weekly. A very common failure is filling in confidence optimistically ("I sort of know it") — be brutal; if you couldn't answer a practice question on it cold, it's a 2, not a 4.

## Template 4 — Feynman Explanation

The Feynman technique is the gold standard for checking understanding: if you can explain something in plain language to someone who doesn't know it, you truly understand it. This template is where the explanation goes, along with the gaps you found.

```
FEYNMAN EXPLANATION — [Concept] — [Date]

THE CONCEPT in one sentence (plain words, no jargon):
  ...

EXPLAIN IT TO A 12-YEAR-OLD (pretend they have no background):
  ...

USE AN ANALOGY from everyday life:
  ... is like ... because ...

THE GAPS (where I got stuck or used vague words):
  1. I couldn't explain ...
  2. I used the word ... without defining it
  3. I don't know why ...

WHAT I'LL DO ABOUT THE GAPS (look up / ask AI / re-read p. X):
  1. ...
  2. ...

FINAL version after fixing gaps:
  ...
```

**How to use it:** Pick a concept you think you know. Write the explanation *without looking at your notes*. Every time you stall or use a jargon word you can't define, that's a gap — write it down. Then fill the gap and re-explain. Done when the explanation is clean, simple, and complete. The mistake students make here is looking at the notes while writing the explanation — that turns Feynman into copying. Write from memory first, catch gaps, *then* check the notes. Also, one real analogy beats three forced ones.

## Template 5 — Elaborative Interrogation

Elaborative interrogation means asking *"why is this true?"* for every fact instead of just memorizing it. Why questions force your brain to connect new facts to things you already know, which makes them stick far better. This template structures that.

```
ELABORATIVE INTERROGATION — [Topic] — [Date]

FACT (as stated in the source): ...
WHY is this true? (mechanism, reason, logic): ...
WHAT does this explain or predict? ...
HOW does it connect to something I already know?
  • I know [prior knowledge] → this connects because ...
  • Counter-example / exception: ... → because ...
IF I had to defend this to a skeptic, my argument: ...
```

**How to use it:** For every key fact or concept in your material, run it through this template. The "WHY" column is the one that matters most — answer it in your own words first, then check the textbook. The more distinct the "why," the better you'll remember it at exam time. The trap: treating this as busywork by writing shallow "whys" like "because that's how it works." Push until the "why" names a cause, a mechanism, or a comparison. If you can't find a real "why" for a fact, that's a sign the fact may be less important than you think.

## Template 6 — Concept Map

A concept map is a diagram of how ideas connect: nodes are concepts, lines are relationships, and the labels on the lines say *how* they relate (e.g., "causes," "is an example of," "contradicts"). It's dual coding — you're storing the information as both words and spatial layout, which gives you two ways to retrieve it.

```
CONCEPT MAP — [Topic] — [Date]

                 ┌───────────────┐
                 │ [Central idea] │
                 └───────┬───────┘
            ┌────────────┼────────────┐
        "causes"     "is a type of"  "relates to"
            ▼            ▼            ▼
     ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐
     │ [Idea A] │  │ [Idea B] │  │ [Idea C] │
     └────┬─────┘  └────┬─────┘  └────┬─────┘
          │ "contrasts with"    "example of"│
          ▼                      ▼
     ┌──────────┐           ┌──────────┐
     │ [Idea D] │           │ [Idea E] │
     └──────────┘           └──────────┘
```

**How to use it:** Put the chapter's central idea in the middle (prompt #23 generates the map as text you can redraw). Add branches for each main concept. Label every line with a relationship word. A good map has 5–15 nodes — if you have 40, you haven't grouped yet. Redraw the map from memory a few days later: that redraw *is* active recall. The biggest mistake is making the map a work of art instead of a retrieval tool — sketch it fast, don't polish it. And don't keep the source open while redrawing; the redraw from memory is where the learning happens.

## Template 7 — Exam Error Log

You will make mistakes on practice questions. The single highest-leverage study habit is logging every mistake and understanding *why* you made it — not just the correct answer. Most students lose the same marks twice because they never diagnose the cause.

```
EXAM ERROR LOG — [Subject] — [Date of practice test]

QUESTION (topic + number): ...
MY ANSWER: ...
CORRECT ANSWER: ...
WHY I GOT IT WRONG (tick one):
  □ Misread the question   □ Didn't know the fact   □ Knew it but couldn't recall under time
  □ Used wrong method       □ Ran out of time        □ Misunderstood the concept
WHAT SPECIFICALLY I'LL DO DIFFERENTLY NEXT TIME: ...
REVIEW THIS ON: [date — slot into your spaced repetition plan]
```

**How to use it:** After every practice test or quiz, fill a row per wrong answer. The "why" matters more than the "what" — it tells you whether to re-learn a concept, practice speed, or read more carefully. Revisit the log before your next test. If the same "why" appears three times, that's your #1 priority. The most common misuse is filling the log but never revisiting it. Schedule a 10-minute look at the log before every practice test. And be honest in the "why" column — "didn't know the fact" and "misread the question" require completely different fixes.

## Template 8 — 24-Hour Recall Drill

This is a structured, time-boxed review you run within 24 hours of learning something new — the window where forgetting is fastest. It's a full active-recall session built around one session of material.

```
24-HOUR RECALL DRILL — [Topic] — Learned on [date], drill on [date]

PHASE 1 (5 min) — BLURT: write everything you remember about the
  topic. No notes. Don't stop to check. Just dump.
PHASE 2 (10 min) — CHECK: compare your blurting to the source.
  Mark ✓ for correct, ✗ for missed, ~ for fuzzy.
PHASE 3 (10 min) — RE-LEARN: for every ✗ and ~, re-read the
  source section and write a one-line fix.
PHASE 4 (5 min) — RECALL AGAIN: re-do the blurting for the ✗/~ items.
  They should now come out clean.
PHASE 5 (2 min) — LOG: add this topic to your spaced repetition
  plan, and note 3 cues for tomorrow's review.

Result summary — Blurting accuracy: [N]% correct.
What I keep forgetting: [list]. Next review: [date].
```

**How to use it:** Within 24 hours of any study session, run this drill. It doesn't take long, but it catches the forgetting curve right at its steepest point — which is exactly when a 10-minute review buys the most memory. Do this after every new topic and your retention will jump visibly. The failure mode is skipping Phase 1 (blurting) because it feels hard — it's supposed to. Blurting is the entire engine of the drill; the later phases just repair what it reveals. Do it within 24 hours; waiting 3 days loses most of the benefit.

## Summarizing & Note-Taking

### Prompt 1 — The Chapter Transformer (Cornell-style summary)

```
You are a study tutor. I'm going to paste a section of {{my textbook or lecture notes}} for {{subject}}.
Convert it into Cornell-style notes:
1. Main ideas as short bullet points in the right column.
2. A left column of 5-8 CUE QUESTIONS that each bullet answers (one question per idea).
3. A 3-sentence summary in plain language.
4. A "muddiest point" line naming the single most confusing part.
Keep it faithful to the source. Do not add facts that aren't there. Use my exact vocabulary where it matters. If the text has jargon, define each term once in parentheses the first time it appears.
```

**How to use:** Paste textbook or lecture text in place of `{{my textbook or lecture notes}}`. This gives you a ready Cornell page — then cover the right column and quiz yourself with the cue questions. Use when you want a clean, structured version of messy notes.

### Prompt 2 — The "Explain Like I'm 12" Summary (Feynman)

```
Explain {{the chapter / idea}} to a 12-year-old who knows nothing about {{subject}}.
Rules:
- No jargon. If you must use a technical term, define it in plain words right after.
- Use one everyday analogy.
- Keep it under 250 words.
- End with one "so what?" sentence: why this matters in the real world.
- Then list the 3 hardest underlying ideas and explain each in one plain sentence.
```

**How to use:** Fastest way to find out if you actually understand. If the AI's plain version makes sense to you, you likely know the material. If it doesn't, re-read the source — you probably had a false sense of understanding.

### Prompt 3 — The 5-Level Zoom Summary

```
Summarize {{the material}} at 5 levels of depth:
Level 1 — One sentence (the whole thing).
Level 2 — One paragraph (the core argument/reasoning).
Level 3 — A 5-bullet outline of the main points.
Level 4 — A detailed outline with sub-points and evidence under each bullet.
Level 5 — The full "exam-ready" version with every key term, definition, and example.
Label each level clearly. Start with Level 1 and end with Level 5.
```

**How to use:** Read Level 1 first to get the gist, then Level 3, then Level 5 to study. This is also a great self-test: cover Levels 3–5 and try to recall them from Level 1.

### Prompt 4 — The Key-Term Extractor

```
From {{the material}}, extract every key term and concept (aim for 10-25).
For each, give:
- Term (exact as it appears)
- One-sentence definition in your own words
- A "memory hook" or mnemonic
- One example from the text
- One related term it connects to
Format as a table. Put this in my notes app.
```

**How to use:** Perfect for a new chapter before you read it — skim terms first to form a mental skeleton, then read. Or run it after reading to build a glossary to review.

### Prompt 5 — The Textbook Page Note-Taker

```
Turn {{the page or passage}} into clean study notes:
1. Title and subtopic headings in hierarchy.
2. Bullet points, not full sentences, for main ideas.
3. Highlight the 2-4 most important ideas with asterisks.
4. Add "TRICKY" labels where the wording is confusing.
5. Add "EXAMPLE" boxes where the text gives an example.
6. Use bold for terms that will appear on the exam.
Output as markdown so it looks clean.
```

**How to use:** Use for a single textbook page or a dense paragraph. The key output is the "TRICKY" flags — those tell you where to spend your attention and which sections to re-read.

### Prompt 6 — The Meeting/Lecture Recap

```
Here is a transcript of {{the lecture / video / meeting}}: [PASTE].
Produce a one-page study recap:
1. The 3 biggest takeaways
2. What the speaker said about the exam / what's most likely to be tested
3. Any questions the lecturer asked the class (these are exam hints)
4. Any homework or reading assigned
5. A "confusing part" note for anything you want me to re-read
Keep it scannable with headings and bullets.
```

**How to use:** After a lecture, paste the transcript or your rough notes. The "questions the lecturer asked" section is pure gold — lecturers tend to repeat exam content as questions in class.

### Prompt 7 — The Diagrams-to-Words Translator (Dual coding)

```
I'm going to describe a diagram/table/graph from {{my textbook}}: [describe it, or paste the caption + surrounding text].
Translate it into words that capture the same information:
1. What the figure shows, in one sentence.
2. The key trend or relationship (e.g., "as X increases, Y decreases").
3. The 2-3 specific data points that matter most.
4. What a student would need to memorize about this figure for an exam.
5. A plain-text version of the figure I could redraw from memory.
```

**How to use:** Diagrams are hard to paste into a chatbot. Describe it or paste its caption/text. This turns visual info into verbal form — which is exactly what you need for a written exam where you can't draw.

## Active Recall & Flashcards

### Prompt 8 — The Atomic Flashcard Maker

```
Create flashcards from {{the material}} for {{subject}}.
Rules:
- One idea per card. If a card would need two questions, split it.
- Front: a question that starts with "What," "Why," "How," "Define," or "Compare."
- Back: a complete answer in 1-3 sentences.
- Add a "hint" line to the back for the most common wrong answer.
- Add a "memory hook" line (mnemonic or analogy) to 10% of cards where it helps.
- Aim for 15-30 cards.
Format: a numbered list, with Front / Back / Hint / Hook on separate lines.
```

**How to use:** The number-one rule: never put a full sentence's worth of text on a card's front. One idea, one card. Paste the output straight into anki, Quizlet, or Notion for drilling.

### Prompt 9 — The Active-Recall Question Generator

```
Based on {{the material}}, generate 15 short-answer questions that test real understanding, not just memory.
Make them progressive:
- 5 recall questions (facts, definitions)
- 5 application questions (apply the idea to a new situation)
- 5 "why/explain" questions (must show reasoning)
Do not provide answers — list the questions only, numbered. I will answer them myself and check against the text.
```

**How to use:** Answer the questions yourself *before* asking the AI for the answers — that's the retrieval practice. Only after you've tried, ask the AI to check your answers against the source.

### Prompt 10 — The Blurting Coach

```
I am going to "blurt" everything I remember about {{topic}} after studying it. Here is my attempt: [PASTE YOUR BLURT].
Compare it to the source material {{the material}} and tell me:
1. What I got right (confirm these)
2. What I missed entirely (list these — these are my gaps)
3. What I got wrong or fuzzy (correct these)
4. The ordering: what I should review first based on my gaps
Be encouraging but accurate. Do not add extra facts beyond the source.
```

**How to use:** After studying, write everything you remember in quick free-form (blurting), then paste it here. The AI's gap list becomes your to-do for the next study burst. This is a form of retrieval practice with instant feedback.

### Prompt 11 — The Fill-in-the-Blank Maker

```
Take {{the material}} and turn the 10 most important statements into fill-in-the-blank questions.
For each:
- The complete original sentence
- The blanked version (replace the key word/phrase with _____)
- 3 plausible wrong options for the blank (distractors)
- The answer
Format as a numbered list. The wrong options should be related to the topic, not random.
```

**How to use:** Great for self-quizzing. Cover the answer column and try to fill the blanks from memory. The distractors also train you to tell similar concepts apart.

### Prompt 12 — The One-Pager Recall Drill

```
Create a one-page "recall sheet" for {{the material}} that has ONLY:
1. The 10 most important terms, each with a one-line cue
2. The 5 key formulas/relationships/chronologies
3. The 3 most common mistakes students make on this topic
4. A blank line under each item for me to fill in from memory
The sheet must be sparse — the point is that I fill in the details myself from memory, then check against the source.
```

**How to use:** Print or copy this sheet, fill it in from memory, then mark gaps against the source. It's the ultimate active-recall tool — the sparseness forces your memory to do the work.

### Prompt 13 — The Staircase Review (progressive difficulty)

```
Create 3 levels of review questions on {{topic}}:
Level 1 (easy): definitions and basic facts — 5 questions.
Level 2 (medium): apply a concept to a scenario — 5 questions.
Level 3 (hard): synthesize/compare/debate — 5 questions.
Number them 1-15 by difficulty. Do not give answers. I will work up the staircase and then ask you to check.
```

**How to use:** Start at Level 1 and climb. When you can clear Level 3, you're exam-ready on that topic. When you can't, that's your study target for the day.

### Prompt 14 — The Interleaved Mixed Review

```
Mix {{topic A}}, {{topic B}}, and {{topic C}} (or more) into ONE randomized set of 20 review questions, not in any logical order — shuffle them.
Do not label the questions by topic. Do not provide answers.
My goal is to practice switching between topics, which builds stronger memory and exam skills.
```

**How to use:** Interleaving (mixing topics) is proven to build stronger long-term memory than blocking (studying one topic fully before the next). Use this the week before an exam to simulate the real test, where topics are mixed.

## Quiz & Self-Testing

### Prompt 15 — The Quiz with Model Answers

```
Create a 10-question quiz on {{the material}}.
- 3 multiple choice, 3 true/false, 2 short answer, 2 "explain the reasoning."
- After the questions, give a modeled answer key with full explanations for each answer (explain *why* each choice is right or wrong).
- Mark which questions would be "easy," "medium," or "hard" on a real exam.
Format the key separately so I can attempt the quiz before reading answers.
```

**How to use:** Do the quiz cold, *then* read the key. The explanations are the learning — they show the reasoning, not just the fact.

### Prompt 16 — The Multiple-Choice Distractor Explainer

```
Here are {{my notes or the chapter}}: [PASTE].
Write 8 multiple-choice questions, each with:
- A clear, correct stem (question)
- 4 options: 1 correct + 3 wrong
- For EACH wrong option, one sentence on why it's wrong and the mistake a student would make to pick it
- For the correct option, one sentence on why it's right
```

**How to use:** The "mistake a student would make" line is the valuable part — wrong answers on exams are designed around common errors, and knowing them protects you. Great self-test before a real exam.

### Prompt 17 — The True/False with Corrections

```
Based on {{the material}}, write 15 true/false statements. Mix in 5 that are subtly false (only wrong by one key word).
For each, state whether it's true or false, and if false, rewrite it to make it true (in one sentence).
Do not put the answer directly under each statement — list all statements first, then all answers in a separate key, so I can test myself.
```

**How to use:** Subtle-false statements are the best training for tricky exam wording. After answering, check the key — any you missed are terms you don't have quite precise enough.

### Prompt 18 — The Speed-Round Drill

```
I'm practicing speed of recall. Create 20 quick-fire questions on {{topic}} that can be answered in 5 seconds or less (one-word or one-phrase answers).
Time me: state the question, then after I answer, immediately tell me if I'm right and give the correct answer if I'm wrong. Keep the pace brisk. Do not explain at length — save explanations for a separate request.
```

**How to use:** Great near exam time. It builds the automatic recall you need for time-pressured multiple-choice sections. You can practice this out loud.

### Prompt 19 — The Exam-Style Paper

```
Create a full practice exam on {{subject}} for {{exam level}}, with:
- The same question formats and marks breakdown as a real exam of this type
- 2 sections: Section A (short answer / MCQ) and Section B (long answer / essay)
- An exam-style mark scheme with points awarded per answer part
- A "typical grade boundaries" note (what score usually gets an A/B/etc.)
Then, after I attempt it, offer to mark my answers against the scheme.
```

**How to use:** The closest you can get to a real exam at home. Do it under timed conditions in one sitting, then use the mark scheme to score yourself and log errors in the Error Log template. Use sparingly — they're high-effort but the best rehearsal.

### Prompt 20 — The "Exit Ticket" Check

```
I just finished studying {{topic}}. Give me a 5-question "exit ticket" (short, quick) to confirm I actually understood before I move on.
Include 1 question that would expose a common misconception. After I answer, tell me whether I'm ready to move on, or what to review first.
```

**How to use:** Run this at the end of every study session. It's a fast gate: pass and move to the next topic; fail and re-learn before moving on. Prevents the classic "studied wrong for 2 hours" trap.

### Prompt 21 — The Flashcard Self-Test Simulator

```
Act as my flashcard deck. You have 20 cards on {{topic}} (invent them based on the material {{the material}}).
Show me one card at a time: first the FRONT only, and wait for my answer before revealing the BACK.
After I answer, tell me if I was right, show the correct back, and ask me to rate my confidence (high/medium/low).
Track how many I get right and give me a summary at the end.
```

**How to use:** This is a fully interactive quiz session — the AI becomes a live flashcard deck. The confidence rating tells you what to review tomorrow (low-confidence cards go in your spaced repetition plan).

## Essay Outlines & Writing

### Prompt 22 — The Essay Outline Builder

```
Here is my essay prompt: {{the prompt}}.
Build a complete outline:
1. A thesis statement (one sentence, argumentative, specific)
2. 3 main body paragraphs, each with:
   - Topic sentence
   - 2-3 supporting points
   - 1 piece of evidence/example for each
   - One sentence linking to the thesis
3. A conclusion paragraph plan (restate thesis, synthesize, why it matters)
4. A brief "what to avoid" note (common pitfalls for this type of prompt)
Do not write the full essay — outline only.
```

**How to use:** Use this to scaffold before writing. The thesis is the most important output — if it's weak, rewrite it before proceeding. Then write the essay yourself using the outline; don't ask the AI to write it for you.

### Prompt 23 — The Counter-Argument Forcer

```
For my thesis "{{your thesis}}" on {{topic}}, find the strongest 2-3 arguments AGAINST it.
For each counter-argument, give:
- The argument in one sentence
- The strongest rebuttal (a response that weakens it)
- Which of my supporting points it threatens most
This is to pressure-test my essay before I write it. Do not soften the counter-arguments.
```

**How to use:** A-grade essays address opposing views. Run this before you write, then weave the rebuttals into your essay — particularly in a paragraph where you acknowledge the counter-argument and refute it.

### Prompt 24 — The Evidence-to-Point Mapper

```
Here are my essay points for {{the prompt}}: [LIST YOUR POINTS].
Here is the evidence I have: [PASTE YOUR SOURCES / QUOTES / DATA].
Match each piece of evidence to the essay point it supports. Flag any point with NO evidence, and any evidence with NO point (this is weak material I should cut or find more for).
For each match, suggest one sentence that connects the evidence to the argument.
```

**How to use:** Run this before you write so you don't discover a hollow paragraph mid-draft. It shows you which claims need more research, and which evidence is orphaned and should be cut.

### Prompt 25 — The Introduction Hook Bank

```
Here's my essay topic: {{the prompt}}. My thesis: {{your thesis}}.
Write 5 different introduction openings (hooks) I can choose from:
1. A surprising statistic or fact
2. A provocative question
3. A short anecdote
4. A striking analogy
5. A bold statement
After each hook, continue with 2 sentences that lead naturally into my thesis. Keep them sample-grade, not gimmicky.
```

**How to use:** Pick the hook that fits your tone and audience — don't force a statistic into a reflective essay. The AI will give you options; you choose. Then write the rest of the intro yourself.

### Prompt 26 — The Plain-English Rewriter

```
Rewrite {{my essay paragraph or draft}} so it's clearer and more concise, while keeping my meaning and voice.
Rules:
- Cut every word that doesn't add meaning.
- Replace vague words with precise ones.
- Break up sentences longer than 25 words.
- Keep my key terminology and argument intact — don't dumb it down.
- Then, in a "what I changed" note, list the 3 biggest changes you made and why.
```

**How to use:** Use after drafting to tighten your writing — not to write for you. The "what I changed" note is the teaching part: each fix is a lesson in clearer academic writing you can apply yourself next time.

### Prompt 27 — The Exam-Essay Timed Plan

```
Here is the essay question: {{the prompt}}. I have {{X}} minutes to answer it in an exam.
Produce a timed plan:
- 0-2 min: quick brainstorm of everything I know on this topic
- 2-5 min: thesis + outline (3 body points)
- 5-40 min: write (with a rough minute budget per paragraph)
- last 5 min: check against the question and fix
Give me the outline I should write from, phrased so I can copy key phrases quickly under time pressure.
```

**How to use:** Practice this under real time pressure — it trains exam discipline, which many students lose. The outline output is your "cheat sheet" for a timed essay; internalize the shape and you can reproduce it for any prompt.

## Exam Prep & Revision

### Prompt 28 — The Mark-Scheme Predictor

```
Based on {{the material}} and the style of {{this course/exam}}, predict:
1. The 5 most likely topics to appear on the exam, ranked by probability.
2. For each, the 2-3 specific sub-topics most likely to be tested.
3. What kind of question each is likely to be (MCQ, short answer, essay, calculation).
4. One "wildcard" topic (less likely, but possible).
Be honest about uncertainty — mark each prediction as high/medium/low confidence.
```

**How to use:** Use this to triage your revision toward what matters most. But don't study ONLY the top topics — treat it as a prioritization helper, not a guarantee.

### Prompt 29 — The Error-Log Analyzer

```
Here is my error log from past practice tests: [PASTE YOUR ERROR LOG ENTRIES].
Analyze it and tell me:
1. My most frequent error type (misreading / not knowing / wrong method / won time, etc.)
2. My weakest topic (most errors per question attempted)
3. A prioritized fix plan: what to review first, and in what order
4. One study strategy that specifically targets my top error type
Give me a concrete plan, not just a summary.
```

**How to use:** Run this after you have at least 10 logged errors. It converts your mistakes into a targeted revision plan — this is the difference between "doing past papers" and actually improving.

### Prompt 30 — The "If I Only Had 3 Days" Blitz

```
I have {{3 days}} until my {{subject}} exam. I have {{N}} chapters to cover and my current weakest areas are {{list}}.
Build a day-by-day blitz plan that:
- Allocates each chapter to a day and time block
- Puts my 2 weakest areas in the morning of each day (best focus)
- Includes 2 self-test slots per day
- Includes 1 spaced-repetition review of each day's earlier material
- Ends day 3 with a full practice paper
Be realistic about hours — don't schedule more than I can actually do.
```

**How to use:** This is the emergency plan. Be honest about hours so the plan is actually executable. Then follow it closely — a good plan you stick to beats a perfect plan you don't.

### Prompt 31 — The Past-Paper Post-Mortem

```
Here is my attempt at a past paper question: [PASTE YOUR ANSWER] and here is the mark scheme: [PASTE MARK SCHEME].
Give me:
1. Which marking points I hit (check them off)
2. Which I missed (the gap)
3. One specific technique to capture the missed marks next time (e.g., "define the term the first line," "include an example," "give both sides")
4. A rewritten model answer to the same question showing the technique
```

**How to use:** Do this after every past-paper question. The "one specific technique" is what you should apply to all future answers. This is deliberate practice — the highest-quality revision you can do.

### Prompt 32 — The Night-Before Checklist

```
Tomorrow is my {{subject}} exam. Make a calm, focused night-before checklist:
1. What to review (only high-yield: my effort log topics, top predictions, formulas/terms)
2. What NOT to do tonight (no new topics, no all-nighter, no doom-scrolling notes)
3. A 20-minute final "closed-book recall" drill to run once
4. What to prepare physically (equipment, water, timing plan for the exam room)
5. A short grounding line to read before the exam
Keep it concise — it should fit on one page.
```

**How to use:** Follow it the night before. The single most useful item is the 20-minute closed-book recall — recall the top topics from memory, check gaps, then stop studying. Sleep beats cramming, every time.

### Prompt 33 — The Marks-Priority Revise

```
Here is my exam structure: {{the exam format, e.g., "40% MCQ, 30% short answer, 30% essay"}} and here are my topic confidence levels: {{list topics with confidence 1-5}}.
Work out where I should put my remaining {{X}} hours:
- Rank every topic by "marks weight × (5 - confidence)" — high marks, low confidence first
- Give me a percentage allocation of my remaining time per topic
- Flag any topic where the effort isn't worth the marks
This is to maximize my expected score, not to be fair to every topic.
```

**How to use:** Run this when you have limited time and many topics. It's the mathematical way to prioritize: study things that are worth many marks AND that you're weak at. It's the opposite of studying your favorite topic — which is what most people do by instinct.

## Memory & Retention Techniques

### Prompt 34 — The Spaced-Repetition Scheduler

```
Here is my list of topics to learn with the date I first learned each: {{list topic: date}}.
Build me a spaced repetition schedule using intervals of: 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days after first learning.
For each topic, output a table of review dates.
Then, for TODAY, list exactly which topics are due and the review method for each (recall first, then check).
```

**How to use:** Run this at the start of each study week and paste the result into Template 2. It turns your growing topic list into a precise "what to review today" answer. This is the single best technique in this pack for long-term retention.

### Prompt 35 — The Memory Palace Builder

```
Help me memorize {{a list of items / the key points}} for {{subject}} using the method of loci (memory palace).
1. Invent a vivid, familiar location (or use one I suggest: {{location}}).
2. Place each item at a distinct spot in that location, with a bizarre, exaggerated, visual image for each (the weirder the better).
3. Walk me through the route so I can visualize each item in order.
4. Give me a recall test: name the spots and ask me to recall the items.
```

**How to use:** Best for ordered lists (chronologies, steps in a process, lists of causes). The bizarre images are the point — boring images don't stick. Use it for lists of 10-20 items; don't force it on everything.

### Prompt 36 — The Chunking Organizer

```
I need to memorize {{a large set of facts / a long list, e.g., a list of terms or steps}}. [PASTE THE LIST].
Organize it into meaningful chunks (3-7 items per chunk) based on:
- Categories or themes
- Ordered sequences or timelines
- Cause-effect relationships
Then give me a mnemonic or catchy acronym for each chunk, and a "hook" sentence that connects the chunks in order.
```

**How to use:** Your working memory holds about 4-7 items at once. Chunking compresses 20 items into 4 chunks of 5, which you can actually hold. Use for anything you'd otherwise brute-force memorize.

### Prompt 37 — The Peg-Word Converter

```
Help me memorize {{a numbered list, e.g., the 10 steps, 12 cranial nerves, etc.}} using the peg-word system.
1. Give me a standard peg-word list (one = sun, two = shoe, three = tree, ...) adapted to the number of items.
2. For each item, create a vivid, interactive image linking the item to its peg word.
3. Present the items in order with their images.
4. Then quiz me: give peg words, ask me to recall the items.
```

**How to use:** For anything with a fixed order and a number (steps, ranks, periods). The peg word system is a proven way to lock numbered lists into memory. It pairs well with Prompt 35.

### Prompt 38 — The Mnemonic Maker

```
Create memorable mnemonics for {{top list of terms/concepts/facts I need to remember}}.
For each:
- A first-letter acronym (if the initial letters form a word, even better)
- An acrostic sentence (where each word's first letter matches the list)
- A rhyme or catchy phrase if it fits
Choose the mnemonic that's easiest for me to recall under exam pressure. Keep each under one line.
```

**How to use:** Use for lists you have to recall in exact order or as a whole. The best mnemonic is the one *you* find memorable — so tweak the AI's suggestions until they feel sticky to you.

### Prompt 39 — The "Learn It Backwards" Revise

```
Help me learn {{the material}} more deeply by studying it backwards:
1. Start with the final conclusion/outcome of the topic.
2. Work backwards: what directly leads to that outcome? And before that?
3. Build a reverse chain until you reach the starting concept.
4. Present this as a numbered reverse-flow list.
5. Then quiz me by giving me the conclusion and asking me to reconstruct the chain forward.
```

**How to use:** Backward learning exposes the logic and dependencies you miss when reading forward. It's excellent for processes, cause-effect chains, and multi-step arguments. It also builds flexible recall, since you now know the material from two directions.

### Prompt 40 — The Weekly Study Planner

```
Here is my week: {{list your commitments, e.g., classes, work, activities, with times}} and here are my study priorities for this week: {{list topics + goals}}.
Build a weekly study plan that:
- Blocks realistic study sessions (45-90 min each) into my free time
- Uses the Pomodoro pattern: 25 min focused, 5 min break (note it in the blocks)
- Schedules my hardest subject in my peak-focus time (morning if I'm a morning person)
- Schedules at least one review session per previously-learned topic

Output as a day-by-day table with time slots.
```

**How to use:** Run this on Sunday for the coming week. Be honest about your free time — plan the *real* you, not an idealized version who studies 8 hours straight. Realistic plans get followed.

### Prompt 41 — The Time-Audit Tracker

```
I want to see where my time actually goes. Ask me these questions one at a time:
1. What time do you wake up and go to bed?
2. List your fixed commitments (classes, work, sports, meals) with times.
3. How much time per day do you spend on phone/social/entertainment?
4. When do you feel most alert and least alert?
Then compute:
- My total free hours per day and per week
- What fraction I currently spend studying versus everything else
- The single biggest "time leak" I could cut
- A realistic daily study capacity based on my energy curve
```

**How to use:** Run this once per month. It grounds your planning in reality. The "biggest time leak" is usually the single highest-leverage change available to you.

### Prompt 42 — The Pomodoro Session Commander

```
Run a Pomodoro study session with me on {{topic}}.
Plan 4 rounds of 25 minutes focused work + 5 minute breaks, then one long 15-minute break.
For each round, tell me exactly what to do:
- Round 1: {{task, e.g., summarize chapter}}
- Round 2: {{task, e.g., make flashcards}}
- Round 3: {{task, e.g., active recall quiz}}
- Round 4: {{task, e.g., review errors}}
During each break, give me a 1-line prompt to step away (stretch, water, look away from screens). At the end, ask me for a 1-line reflection on what I got done.
```

**How to use:** This is a live productivity session — you and the AI work together through a focused block. Decide your 4 tasks first (use the placeholders), then run it. The breaks are not optional; they're what make the focus sustainable.

### Prompt 43 — The Goal Breakdown Builder

```
Help me plan for {{my exam / big project}} on {{date}}.
1. Break the whole syllabus/work into small, specific tasks (each 30-90 min).
2. Estimate how many sessions each task needs.
3. Work backwards from the exam date to build a timeline.
4. Mark the 3 tasks that are most likely to be skipped (hard or boring) and suggest one way to make each less daunting.
5. Output this as a checklist I can tick off.
```

**How to use:** Big goals feel overwhelming; small tasks don't. Run this when you need to turn "study for exams" into an actionable checklist. The "likely to be skipped" warnings help you pre-empt procrastination.

### Prompt 44 — The Two-Minute Task List

```
Give me a list of all the tiny, under-2-minute study tasks I can do during short gaps (between classes, waiting, commuting) on {{subject}} today.
Examples: review 3 flashcards, recall one diagram from memory, define 5 terms out loud, plan tomorrow's study session, redraw one concept map.
Generate 10 such micro-tasks specific to my current topics {{topics}}, each described in one line.
```

**How to use:** Use the small dead moments in your day. Ten minutes of micro-tasks across a day adds up to real review time, and it keeps the material warm between study sessions.

### Prompt 45 — The Distraction-Proofing Plan

```
I get distracted when studying {{subject}}. Help me build a distraction-proof setup.
1. Ask me about my main distractions (phone notifications, social media, noise, people, environment) — ask one at a time.
2. For each distraction, give one concrete fix (e.g., phone in another room, site blocker, noise-cancelling, specific spot).
3. Draft a "study ritual" — a fixed 3-step routine I do every time before I start studying (e.g., clear desk, set timer, put phone away).
4. Write a 1-line mantra I can repeat when I feel the urge to check my phone.
```

**How to use:** Environment beats willpower. Run this once and commit to the setup. The study ritual becomes a trigger that gets your brain into focus mode faster each time.

## Learning Difficult Concepts

### Prompt 46 — The Puzzle-Piece Explainer

```
I'm struggling with {{the difficult concept}}. Explain it using a step-by-step "puzzle-piece" approach:
1. First explain the SIMPLEST version of the idea (the core kernel).
2. Then add each layer of complexity one at a time, checking I follow between layers.
3. After each layer, ask me a quick check question to confirm I got it before moving on.
4. At the end, ask me to explain it back to you in my own words.
Wait for my answers between steps — don't dump everything at once.
```

**How to use:** This is interactive — don't paste-and-run, engage with it. It breaks a wall of confusion into digestible steps, each verified before moving on. Best for genuinely hard, layered concepts.

### Prompt 47 — The First-Principles Rebuild

```
Explain {{the difficult concept}} from first principles — assume I know nothing about it.
1. Identify the fundamental building blocks (the axioms, base assumptions) the concept rests on.
2. Build up the concept from those blocks, one logical step at a time.
3. Whenever you introduce a term, define it in plain language.
4. Show where the concept connects to something I likely already know ({{my background}}).
5. End with a one-sentence "essence" of the concept.
```

**How to use:** When a concept is tangled, going back to its foundations often untangles it. This works best for technical subjects where a term hides a chain of prior knowledge.

### Prompt 48 — The Analogy First-Aid Kit

```
I don't understand {{the difficult concept}}. Explain it using 3 different analogies from completely different domains (e.g., a kitchen, a city, a sport, a video game, a machine).
For each analogy:
- The analogy itself
- Where the analogy fits well
- Where the analogy breaks down (important — so I don't over-extend it)
Then recommend which analogy is best for building an accurate mental model, and why.
```

**How to use:** Different analogies will click differently for you. The "where it breaks down" note is the difference between a helpful analogy and a dangerously wrong one — always read it.

### Prompt 49 — The Worked-Example Walkthrough

```
Here is a problem I'm stuck on: {{the problem or question}} — and here's my attempt: {{your work so far}}.
1. Identify exactly where my attempt goes wrong (be specific — point to the step).
2. Show the correct full solution, step by step, explaining the reasoning at each step.
3. Give me a SIMILAR problem to try myself (with the answer hidden) so I can practice.
4. After I attempt it, check my work and give feedback.
```

**How to use:** This is the classic worked-example method. The "where my attempt goes wrong" step is crucial — comparing your attempt to the correct one is how you learn from mistakes. For maths and problem-heavy subjects, this is your workhorse prompt.

### Prompt 50 — The "Tutor Me" Session

```
Act as a patient tutor for {{the difficult topic}}. I'm a {{level}} student.
Use the Socratic method: teach by asking me questions, not by lecturing.
1. Start by asking me one question to assess what I already know about {{topic}}.
2. Based on my answer, guide me step by step with questions that lead me to understand the concept myself — only give direct explanations when I'm truly stuck.
3. Correct my misconceptions gently and immediately when they appear.
4. End with a recap quiz of 3 questions to check I've got it.
Wait for my answer after each question. Do not lecture — I need to think.
```

**How to use:** This is the highest-value prompt in the pack for difficult material. You must actually engage — answer the questions out loud or in writing. It trains you to reason, not just memorize, and it will reveal gaps in your understanding a lecture never would.

## Science Pack (e.g., Biology / Chemistry / Physics)

**The setup:** You have a chapter on cellular respiration in biology. You paste the chapter into the chat.

**Prompt used:** Prompt 1 — The Chapter Transformer, plus Prompt 8 — The Atomic Flashcard Maker.

**What you get back (example shape):**

```
Cornell-style notes for "Cellular Respiration"

CUE QUESTIONS:
- What is the overall equation for aerobic respiration?
- Where does glycolysis take place?
- What is the role of the electron transport chain?
- What are the 3 stages and their net ATP yields?

NOTES:
• Aerobic respiration: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ~30-32 ATP
• Stage 1 Glycolysis (cytoplasm): glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP, 2 NADH
• Stage 2 Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix): produces 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2
• Stage 3 Electron transport chain (inner membrane): oxidative phosphorylation, ~28 ATP
• Final electron acceptor: oxygen → forms water

SUMMARY:
Cellular respiration breaks down glucose to produce ATP in three stages. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and yields little ATP; the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain in the mitochondria yield most. Oxygen is essential because it accepts electrons in the final stage.

MUDDIEST POINT: Why the electron transport chain produces so many more ATP than the other stages.
```

**How to study it:** Cover the NOTES column, answer the CUE questions out loud. Then feed the chapter into Prompt 8 for 15-20 atomic flashcards on the three stages, their locations, and ATP yields. Test yourself tomorrow and in 3 days using your spaced repetition plan.

## Maths Pack (e.g., Algebra / Calculus / Statistics)

**The setup:** You're stuck on integration by parts.

**Prompt used:** Prompt 49 — The Worked-Example Walkthrough, plus Prompt 13 — The Staircase Review.

**What you get back (example shape):**

```
Your attempt: ∫ x·e^x dx — you tried u = e^x, dv = x dx (wrong choice)
Where it goes wrong: Choosing u = e^x and dv = x dx makes the new integral (∫ (x²/2)·e^x dx) HARDER than the original. The rule: choose u = the part that gets simpler when differentiated (here, x), and dv = the part that's easy to integrate (here, e^x).

Correct solution:
1. Let u = x, dv = e^x dx → du = dx, v = e^x
2. ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du
3. = x·e^x − ∫ e^x dx
4. = x·e^x − e^x + C
5. = e^x(x − 1) + C

Practice problem for you: ∫ x·sin(x) dx  (answer hidden — attempt it, then ask me to check)
```

**How to study it:** Attempt the practice problem yourself, then ask the AI to check it. Then use Prompt 13 to build a staircase of integration questions (easy → medium → hard) — interleave a few integration-by-parts questions with other integration types. Log any errors in the Error Log.

## Business Pack (e.g., Economics / Business Studies / Marketing)

**The setup:** You need to understand and remember the four Ps of marketing for an exam.

**Prompt used:** Prompt 35 — The Memory Palace Builder, plus Prompt 17 — The True/False with Corrections.

**What you get back (example shape):**

```
Memory Palace for the Four Ps (use your kitchen as the palace)
1. PRODUCT — imagine a product (a bottle) sitting in the fridge, with a price tag on it... wait, that's the next one. Place the PRODUCT on the fridge door.
2. PRICE — a huge glowing dollar-sign as the fridge handle.
3. PLACE — a map of the world stuck to the fridge (distribution channels).
4. PROMOTION — a megaphone on the kitchen table, blaring an ad.

Recall route: enter kitchen → fridge door (Product) → handle (Price) → map (Place) → table (Promotion).

TRUE/FALSE set (sample):
1. "Promotion includes only advertising." → FALSE — promotion includes PR, sales promotion, personal selling, direct marketing too.
2. "Price is the only P that generates revenue." → TRUE — the other three are costs.
3. "Place refers only to physical stores." → FALSE — place includes online channels and distribution.
```

**How to study it:** Walk the kitchen route three times picturing the images, then cover the list and recall the four Ps in order. Do the true/false set tomorrow from memory. Add "which P generates revenue" as a cue in your Cornell notes — it's a classic exam trap.

If you're in this situation, focus on the highest-marks topics, do one full practice paper, and sleep before the exam; a rested brain outperforms a crammed one

# FAQ

**Q1. Which AI should I use with these prompts?**
Any modern chatbot works — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, or a free option. The prompts are written to be model-agnostic. If one model gives you a weak answer, try another or just add a follow-up line like *"format this as a table"* or *"be more specific."*

**Q2. Can I paste my whole textbook in?**
You can paste a chapter or a few pages at a time. For long texts, paste the material in its own message first, then reference it (*"using the notes I just pasted, ..."*) instead of re-pasting it into every prompt. Some models have input limits — if your paste fails, split the chapter in half.

**Q3. Will this write my essays for me?**
No — and it shouldn't. The essay prompts produce *outlines, hooks, and feedback*, not finished essays. Writing the essay yourself is the entire point: that's where you learn, and it's also what your school expects. Use the AI to scaffold and critique, never to ghostwrite. Submitting AI-written work as your own is academic dishonesty and will hurt you far more than it helps.

**Q4. How is this different from just asking the AI "help me study"?**
Generic requests produce generic output: a wall of text you won't remember. Each prompt here is engineered for a specific cognitive technique, has a defined output shape (table, flashcards, outline, error analysis), and tells you *how* to use the result. It's the difference between a tool and a toy.

**Q5. Do these actually help with exam results?**
The techniques behind them — active recall, spaced repetition, retrieval practice, interleaving, elaborative interrogation, the Feynman method — are among the most strongly supported findings in learning science. The prompts are just a way to actually *do* those techniques consistently. What the AI gives you only helps if you do the recall work yourself; the pack is designed to force that.

**Q6. How much time does each prompt take?**
Most prompts produce a usable output in one message (under a minute of your time). The real time is in *using* the output — drilling flashcards, answering quizzes, filling templates — which is exactly the time that should be studying. A typical good session: 20 minutes of prompt-driven prep + 40 minutes of active recall work.
