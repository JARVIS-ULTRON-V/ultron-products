# Real Estate Lead Gen AI Prompt Pack

### 70+ outcome-driven prompts to generate listings, nurture leads and automate follow-ups.

Real estate conversations die in the gaps. A lead emails at 8:40pm and hears back Monday. A buyer asks one question about a tax bill and goes quiet for six weeks. A seller wonders what happened to the weekend open house and never gets an honest debrief. This pack closes those gaps with prompts you can paste into any AI assistant and adapt in under five minutes.

Inside are **70+ numbered, outcome-driven prompts** covering the workflows that actually move inventory: writing listing descriptions and MLS copy, marketing open houses, nurturing new buyers, pre-approved buyers and first-time buyers separately, keeping sellers informed, handling price objections, explaining valuations, replying fast to inbound leads, writing social content, activating referral partners and past clients, reactivating old database contacts, and carrying clients from contract to close and review.

You also get **12 ready-to-use artifact templates** — email sequences, call scripts, social post sets, an Instagram carousel outline, an SMS/WhatsApp follow-up flow, a valuation conversation guide and a review request message — each one laid out with exact sections, exact columns and exact cadence so you can drop a client's real details in and send.

Everything is organized by workflow. Nothing is theoretical. Every prompt names its inputs as `{{placeholders}}`, tells you where to use it, and tells you what a good output looks like so you can tell good copy from plausible filler.

**Format:** PDF + Notion. The PDF is for printing and sharing with your team; the Notion version is for copying directly into your CRM notes, your listing templates, and your email/SMS workflows.

## Compliance & Accuracy

> **Read this before you use a single prompt in this pack.**
>
> Every prompt in this pack produces *draft* material that a licensed agent must review, edit, and approve before it goes anywhere. AI writes fast and confidently, and confidence is not accuracy. You are accountable for every word that reaches a consumer, a client, or a regulator.

### Rules that apply to every output in this pack

1. **Local advertising rules first.** State, provincial, and municipal real estate advertising rules vary and change. Many jurisdictions now regulate marketing copy, brokerage name usage, team branding, and comparative market statements directly. Before publishing anything generated here, confirm the copy complies with the advertising rules of the state or province where the property sits, with your brokerage's standards, and with MLS rules for the board covering that property.
2. **Fair housing guidance is non-negotiable.** Generated copy must never describe a property or area in ways that steer, target, or exclude protected classes — race, color, religion, national origin, familial status, disability, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, or any other class protected under applicable fair housing law. This includes the language people use casually: "good schools," "safe neighborhood," "ideal for families," "quiet building," "Christian-friendly area," "perfect for a young couple," or "not great for kids." Replace subjective neighborhood claims with verifiable, factual attributes — square footage, year built, lot size, commute time to a named landmark, school assignment (only if you can source it and you are permitted to name it).
3. **Respect platform-specific character limits.** MLS public remarks fields, email subject lines, SMS segments, Instagram captions, LinkedIn posts, and X posts all have hard limits, and several count characters differently (some count Unicode code points, some count grapheme clusters, some truncate). The prompts ask the AI to target specific lengths — verify with a character counter before publishing, and never let a platform auto-truncate a compliance disclaimer or a required disclosure.
4. **Review all AI-generated claims about property features before publishing.** This is the rule agents skip and then regret. AI will happily invent a finished basement, a new roof, a three-car garage, a water view, a "$40,000 kitchen renovation," central air, or a "quiet cul-de-sac" because those are plausible details, not because anyone verified them. Every claim about structure, systems, appliances, renovations, permits, utilities, water, sewer, storage, parking, views, orientation, and dimensions must be checked against the listing sheet, the MLS record, seller disclosure documents, and your own inspection of the property. Delete any claim you cannot source.
5. **Accuracy also covers numbers.** Square footage, lot size, year built, bed and bath counts, HOA dues, tax figures, utility costs, days on market, comparable sales, and price-per-square-foot must come from verified records, not from the model's assumptions.
6. **Never let a prompt invent legal, tax, lending, or inspection conclusions.** These prompts do not replace a licensed attorney, a tax advisor, a lender, or a home inspector. If a prompt's output contains a legal or financial conclusion, rewrite it into a statement of fact or a referral.
7. **Consent and channel rules still apply.** SMS and WhatsApp templates in this pack assume the recipient has opted in and that the platform's messaging rules are satisfied. Send commercial messages only where consent exists, honor opt-outs immediately, and include required sender identification.
8. **Disclosure obligations are yours.** If your market requires seller disclosure statements, agency disclosures, brokerage advertising statements, equal housing opportunity language, or offer-of-compensation language in marketing, add them and place them exactly where the rule requires. This pack does not know your jurisdiction and does not guess.

### Pre-publish accuracy checklist

Run this checklist on every AI-generated asset before it goes to a client, a lead, or a public channel. If any row fails, fix it or cut it.

| Check | What you are verifying | Typical failure to catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feature claims | Every feature, finish, system and renovation exists and is disclosed | "Brand-new HVAC," "custom built-ins," "solar panels" |
| Dimensions | Beds, baths, above/below grade, sqft, lot size, parking | Model rounds 1,840 sqft to "nearly 2,000" |
| Financial figures | Price, HOA dues, taxes, utility estimates, price per sqft | Model estimates taxes from a neighboring street |
| Location descriptions | Fair housing compliance, no steering, no safety or school claims | "Family-friendly," "safe," "good schools" |
| Comparative claims | No "best," "cheapest," "highest ROI" without substantiation | "Best value in the neighborhood" |
| Compliance language | Required disclosure, brokerage identification, offer-of-compensation language | Missing equal housing statement |
| Character limits | Fits the target channel without truncation | MLS remark field cut mid-disclosure |
| Personal details | Correct names, pronouns if used, correct pronouns for pronouns used, correct contacts | Mixed up client names between clients |
| Links and CTAs | Tracking links resolve, booking links are current | Expired listing link in a nurture sequence |
| Tone and accuracy of urgency | Claims like "last chance" or "price dropping tomorrow" are true | Invented scarcity on a stale listing |

## How to use this pack

### The five-step routine

| Step | What you do | Time it takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Pick the workflow | Find the category heading that matches the job in front of you — listing copy, buyer nurture, objection handling, closing | 30 seconds |
| 2. Fill in the placeholders | Replace every `{{placeholder}}` with real, verified detail from your records | 2–3 minutes |
| 3. Paste and generate | Paste the prompt into your AI assistant of choice, with your own context or notes appended | 30 seconds |
| 4. Verify against the source | Check every factual claim against the listing sheet, disclosure documents, and CRM record. Fix or cut what you cannot source | 2–3 minutes |
| 5. Adapt to your voice and market | Adjust tone, local terminology, greeting, and call-to-action. Read it out loud once before it sends | 1 minute |

**Budget five minutes per asset.** The AI does the drafting; you do the verifying, the editing, and the sending. That division is what keeps the output both fast and safe.

### Five habits that make the output usable

1. **Give the AI your voice.** Paste three of your own past emails or posts before the prompt: *"Here are three examples of how I write. Match this voice."* Generic output drops away fast.
2. **One asset per generation.** Do not ask for a 5-email sequence and 12 social posts in one pass. Separate generations produce sharper, more usable copy.
3. **Ask for options, not a single answer.** Add "Give me 3 options with different tones" and pick deliberately. You will edit anyway; starting from three options is faster.
4. **Keep the AI honest.** If a prompt needs a fact you do not have, tell the model to write around it with a clearly marked placeholder like `[VERIFY: kitchen remodel date]` rather than inventing it. Delete the bracketed items before publishing.
5. **Save what works.** When an output lands, drop the prompt and the result into your Notion page for that workflow. Within a month you will have your own house style — and a much faster next listing.

### What "good" looks like, in one line

Good copy is specific, verifiable, written in the client's language, and ends in exactly one clear next step. If a draft is vague ("breathtaking home in a great neighborhood"), long, or ends without a request, it is not ready to send.

| # | Touch | Send | Subject line | Core message | Single CTA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Launch announcement | Day 0 | `New on the market: {{beds}} bed, {{baths}} bath at {{property_address}}` | Verified facts, strongest feature, one lifestyle detail | Book a showing |

| 4 | Positioning / value | Day 14 | `Why buyers are noticing {{property_address}}` | Comparison framing using verified data only | Request a private tour |
| 5 | Last-call push | Day 30 | `Still available: {{property_address}}` | Current status, feedback received, urgency that is true | Book a showing this week |

**Touch 1 body skeleton:**
> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> I just photographed a home that came up in {{neighborhood}} that I think is worth your attention.
>

**How to use:** Paste the skeleton into your email template and let the AI in this pack fill each touch from verified listing data. Send Touch 1 to the whole list, Touches 2–5 to anyone who opened or clicked; stop the sequence immediately on any reply, showing request, or "remove me" signal.

| 6 | Neighborhood guide | Day 14 | All | Build value while staying factual | Pick your 2–3 areas |

### Template 5 — Past-Client Referral Ask

**Use when:** a transaction closes, and then periodically with past clients who had a good experience.
**Cadence:** Closing-week thank-you, Day 45, Day 90, then annually on the anniversary of their closing.

| # | Touch | Send | Channel | Ask | Permission step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Closing thank-you | Closing week | Handwritten card or email | None — thanks only | None |
| 2 | How did we do? | Day 30 | Email or text | 1-minute satisfaction survey | "Is now a good time?" |
| 3 | Referral ask | Day 45 | Email or text | "Do you know anyone who is thinking of selling?" | Offer a private referral option |
| 4 | Specific review ask | Day 90 | Email | Review request for the platform | Offer a review link directly |
| 5 | Anniversary check-in | Closing anniversary | Text or card | Annual home-value update + light referral ask | Ask how the year went |

**Touch 3 body skeleton:**
> Hi {{past_client_first_name}},
>
> It has been a few weeks since you closed on {{property_address}} — I hope the move has gone well.
>
> I have a simple question, and "no" is a perfectly good answer: **do you know anyone who is thinking of selling in the next 6–12 months?**
>
> If it is not someone you would want to work with, that is completely fine. I am only asking because you are easy to reach and I do a good job with the people I work with.
>
> If someone comes to mind, I will take care of the first conversation — no awkward intros required. You would simply give them my name and number.
>
> Would you be comfortable with that?
>
> {{agent_name}} · {{agent_phone}}

**How to use:** Send Touch 3 only to clients whose file was genuinely smooth and who received good communication. Ask for one referral, not "anyone you know." Never offer a gift that your brokerage's rules or the referral partner's rules prohibit.
**What good looks like:** The ask is low-pressure and specific, gives the client an easy out, and doesn't make them vouched for.

### Template 6 — Call Script: Listing Appointment

**Use when:** you have a seller lead and want to book the in-person listing consultation.
**Cadence:** single call, 12–20 minutes.

| Section | Purpose | Say (adapt to your voice) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Opening | Name, permission, reason for the call | "Hi {{seller_first_name}}, this is {{agent_name}} with {{brokerage_name}}. Do you have 15 minutes? I don't want to catch you mid-task." |
| 2. Context | What you already know, so they don't repeat it | "When we spoke on {{date}}, you mentioned you're deciding whether to sell now or wait — has anything changed since then?" |
| 3. Timeline | Establish urgency without pressuring | "What would make this decision urgent for you — a move, a life change, a deadline?" |
| 4. Outcome | Define what a good result looks like | "If we did this, what would you want the sale to accomplish?" |
| 5. Numbers | Surface the price conversation early | "Have you had a sense of what comparable homes nearby have been doing, or would you like me to bring that back to you?" |
| 6. Objection check | Surface the real hesitation | "What would make you feel this was the wrong call to make?" |
| 7. Pricing conversation | Explain method, not a number yet | "I price by comparing recent sales, current competition and how long the market takes to absorb a home like yours. I'd want to walk you through the specifics before giving you a number." |
| 8. Logistics | Practical details | "What would need to happen in your home before photos? What's your ideal timeline for being out?" |
| 9. Close | Set the next step explicitly | "Based on what you've told me, let me put together the pricing and a launch plan for {{property_address}}. Can I email it to you by {{date}}? And can we meet Tuesday at {{time}} to walk through the house?" |
| 10. Confirm | Lock it down in writing | Send a same-day email confirming what you agreed and what happens next. |

**How to use:** Run this script as a live-call prep doc, not a script to read. Cover sections 2, 3, 4 and 7 from the prep prompt first so you can spend the call on their answers instead of your questions.
**What good looks like:** You leave the call with a confirmed appointment date, a confirmed email follow-up, and a clear picture of the seller's motivation and timeline.

### Template 7 — Call Script: Price Objection

**Use when:** a seller reacts badly to your recommended list price.
**Cadence:** live call, 20–30 minutes, always followed by a written summary.

| Step | Purpose | Say (adapt) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Pause | Let the emotion surface | "I can hear this isn't what you expected. Tell me more." |
| 2. Reflect | Prove you heard them | "You're concerned that {{list_price}} is above what the market will actually pay." |
| 3. Diagnose | Find the real anchor | "What number were you thinking of, and where did that come from?" |
| 4. Evidence | Present the data | "Here's what I based it on: {{comparable_1}}, {{comparable_2}}, {{comparable_3}} — each one within {{radius}} and within {{date_window}}." |
| 5. Strategy | Explain the plan, not just the number | "If we list at {{original_price}} and it goes quiet, we adjust in steps and end up advertising a lower number publicly. If we list at {{recommended_price}}, we protect the price while we test the market." |
| 6. Consequence | Make the cost concrete | "Every week we sit at a price that's too high costs us {{market_context}} and pushes us into the next tier of buyers." |
| 7. Options | Give real choices | "Option A: list at {{recommended_price}}. Option B: list at {{higher_price}} for {{number_of_days}} days and then adjust. Which one do you want?" |
| 8. Confirm | Write it down | "Let me put both options in an email with the comparable sales so you have the numbers in front of you." |

**How to use:** Prepare the comparable sales with the valuation prompts in this pack before the call. Never argue with a seller's number — diagnose where the anchor came from, then let the data speak.
**What good looks like:** The seller makes the decision with a strategy attached, and the follow-up email includes the comps so the reasoning survives after the call ends.

### Template 11 — Valuation Conversation Guide

**Use when:** a seller wants to know what their home is worth before deciding to list.
**Cadence:** one 30–45 minute conversation, followed by a written CMA summary within 24 hours.

| Step | Purpose | Agent says | Client hears |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Frame | Set expectations, remove pressure | "This isn't an appraisal and I won't give you a number in the first five minutes. First I'll tell you how I price, then we'll look at what the evidence says." | A process, not a sales pitch |
| 2. Context | Understand the property | "Tell me about the home — updates, what you've changed, what you'd want to change." | Their knowledge is valued |
| 3. Method | Explain your pricing approach | "I look at recent sales nearby, at what's competing with you right now, and at how fast homes like yours are selling. Those three things give me a range, not a single number." | Logic behind the price |

| 8. Risks | Be honest | "The risk at the lower end is {{risk}}. The risk at the upper end is {{risk_2}}." | Trust through candor |
| 9. Next step | Close | "Do you want a listing agreement, or would you like me to send the written comparison and check back in {{timeframe}}?" | A no-pressure next step |

**How to use:** Run the "explain my valuation method" prompt before the conversation so you can speak to your process without thinking. Bring the comps on screen or on paper.
**What good looks like:** The seller understands the method well enough to explain it to a spouse, and the conversation ends with either a listing agreement or a scheduled check-in — never a vague "I'll get back to you."


**How to use:** Send within 24 hours of the showings while the detail is fresh, and attach the anonymized notes. When the feedback is unflattering, deliver it in the first third of the email rather than burying it.
**What good looks like:** The inconvenient theme is included and tied to an action; the feedback reads as transcribed, not softened; the recommendation has a deadline; the seller is not left guessing what you want them to do; no visitor is identifiable.

### 5.1 — "It's Too Expensive"

```
A buyer said the price at {{property_address}} is too high. I want to understand which price they mean before I defend any number.

Buyer and their exact words: {{buyer_name}} · {{buyer_objection_words}}
What they are comparing against: {{comparison_reference}} · their stated budget: {{budget_range}} · their monthly target: {{monthly_payment_target}}
Verified features and what they actually cost: {{property_features}} · {{recurring_costs}}
Verified comparables: {{comparable_sales}}
Market data on the gap between list and sale price: {{market_gap_data}}
Agent name and phone: {{agent_name}}, {{agent_phone}}

Write:
1. A diagnostic question sequence, 4 questions in order, that separates four different meanings of "too expensive": total price versus monthly payment, price versus value relative to comparables, price versus their budget ceiling, and price versus other homes they liked. For each question, note what each answer tells me and what I would do differently.

2. A spoken response, 4 to 6 sentences, that reflects their concern, identifies which of the four meanings it is, and responds to that one only. Include the one number that matters most for that interpretation. If the answer is "it genuinely does not fit their budget", say so plainly and offer to keep looking rather than pushing.

3. A written follow-up email, 100–140 words, that handles the most likely interpretation, gives one verified piece of evidence, and asks one question.

4. A one-line rule for when the honest answer is "this is over budget" and the right move is to walk away — and what to offer instead.

Rules: no discounts, no pressure tactics, no fake scarcity, no fair-housing characterizations, and no claiming the home is worth more than the market supports. If I lack the comparable or market data, write [VERIFY: ...].
```

**How to use:** Use the four questions on the call before writing any reply — most "too expensive" objections are a budget ceiling or a monthly-payment misunderstanding, not a valuation argument. Record the interpretation in the CRM so the next call starts where this one ended.
**What good looks like:** The questions isolate one specific meaning rather than arguing about price generally; the spoken response uses one number that matters to that interpretation; the email gives evidence rather than adjectives; the walk-away rule is explicit, because protecting the relationship matters more than one listing.

### 5.2 — "We Need to Think About It"

```
A buyer saw {{property_address}} and said they need to think about it. I want to respect that while still making sure I understand the real blocker.

Buyer: {{buyer_name}} · what they said: {{buyer_objection_words}} · what they said they liked: {{what_they_liked}} · what they hesitated on: {{hesitations}}

Write:
1. A response script for the moment they say it, 4 to 6 sentences. It should: accept the request without flinching ("that makes sense"), name the two or three specific things they reacted to so they feel heard accurately, ask what specifically they want to think through, and offer a concrete way to help them decide — reviewing the comparable sales together, a second look with a contractor's eye, or a quick check on a specific number.

2. Three questions that surface the real blocker without arguing: one about what needs to be true for them to move, one about who else has an opinion, one about what would make them walk away entirely.

3. A follow-up plan as a markdown table: timing | channel | message angle | what I send. Include a same-week email, a 10-day check-in, and a 25-day "should I keep sending this home" message.

4. A one-line rule for when the right move is to stop pushing and let the listing agent know this buyer is thinking, so the seller is not told there is an offer.

Rules: no pressure, no "the market won't wait", no fabricated competing buyers, no fair-housing characterizations, and never repeat a follow-up the buyer already ignored twice.
```

**How to use:** Send the follow-up on the schedule in the table and log every touch. If the buyer goes quiet after the second touch, close the loop with the seller honestly — "still interested, thinking" is not an offer.
**What good looks like:** The response accepts the request without arguing; the questions find a decision factor rather than a stall; the follow-up plan has specific angles rather than "just checking in"; the rule prevents you from reporting enthusiasm as an offer; no buyer receives the same message twice.

## Pricing & Valuation Conversations

### Prompt 1: Explain your comparative market analysis (CMA) to a seller

```
{{seller_name}} is deciding whether to list {{property_address}}. They have never seen a comparative market analysis before. Explain how a CMA works in plain language that a non-agent can follow.

Frame it as a method, not a number. Cover:
1. What a CMA is and is not (a valuation tool, not an official appraisal).
2. The three inputs I use: recent closed sales (comps), active competition currently on the market, and absorption rate (how fast homes like theirs sell).
```

## Transaction Management & Closing

### Prompt 32: Contract-sent celebration and next steps

```
{{buyer_name}} and {{seller_name}} just signed a contract on {{property_address}} at {{contract_price}}. Send a celebratory but professional message to both parties confirming the next steps.

Include:
1. Congratulations — genuine and warm — and name the specific achievement (their accepted offer / successful negotiation).
2. What just happened in plain language: the contract is signed, it's now in escrow, and here's what happens next.
3. The timeline of what happens next: earnest money deposit ({{emd_date}}), inspection period ({{inspection_deadline}}), appraisal, financing ({{financing_deadline}}), and closing ({{closing_date}}).
4. What each side needs to do now (provide docs, schedule inspection, etc.).
5. Reassurance of your availability: "I'll be managing every step and I'll keep you posted at each milestone."
6. Keep it clear and structured but warm — under 220 words.
```

**How to use:** Send this immediately when a contract is ratified. This is the moment you lock in trust and set expectations so surprises don't derail the deal.
**What good looks like:** Both parties understand the roadmap and their role in it, and they feel taken care of. The deal proceeds without confusion at each milestone.

### Prompt 33: Inspection contingency explanation

```
The inspection on {{property_address}} came back with findings ({{summary_of_findings}}). Explain the inspection contingency to {{client_name}} in plain language, and outline the options.

Include:
1. What the inspection contingency means in practical terms: it gives the buyer the right to review findings, negotiate, or walk away.
2. What the findings actually are — translate the inspector's report into plain language for the client (materials, severity, estimated impact).
3. The options:
   - Accept the property as-is and proceed.
   - Request the seller make specific repairs ({{requested_repairs}}).
   - Request a credit or price adjustment for the issues.
   - Walk away if a material issue makes the deal untenable (only if genuinely warranted).
4. Timeline: how many days remain to respond ({{days_remaining}}), and who needs to act by when.
5. My recommendation and reasoning, clearly labeled as my view, with the client making the final decision.
6. Keep it calm, clear, and empowering — under 250 words.
```

**How to use:** Use whenever inspection findings arrive — the most stressful moment in a transaction. Send this promptly and follow up with a call so the client isn't left to panic alone.
**What good looks like:** The client understands what each finding means and the real choices they have, and they make an informed decision calmly — rather than reacting emotionally to the report.

### Prompt 34: Appraisal coming in

```
The appraisal on {{property_address}} came in at {{appraisal_value}} — and there's one of three outcomes: it matched the contract price, it came in low, or it came in high. Write the message to {{client_name}} explaining where we are and why it matters.

Cover whichever case applies:
1. Appraisal at contract price: the clean case — the lender is satisfied, the deal proceeds as planned. Reassure and outline the remaining steps.
2. Appraisal below contract price: explain what this means (the lender will only finance the appraised value, so the gap is {{gap_amount}}). Lay out the options: renegotiate price, bridge the gap with a higher down payment, challenge the appraisal, or walk away. Be clear about which are realistic.
3. Appraisal above contract price: celebrate it — the buyer has instant equity and the deal is in good shape.

For all cases: explain what an appraisal is and is not, note that it protects the buyer and lender, and state the next step clearly. Keep it calm, factual, and under 240 words.
```

**How to use:** Send this the moment you have the appraisal result and follow up by phone. Appraisal surprises derail deals when they're explained poorly — a clear, early explanation keeps it on track.
**What good looks like:** The client understands the appraisal's role, what their options are, and the recommended path — and feels confident in the decision rather than blindsided by the number.

### Prompt 35: Final walkthrough prep

```
We're {{days}} days from closing on {{property_address}}. Write a message to {{buyer_name}} preparing them for the final walkthrough.

Include:
1. What the walkthrough is for: confirming the property is in the agreed condition, that agreed-upon repairs are done, and that it's being delivered as promised — not a chance to renegotiate.
2. What to bring: the inspection report, the repair list, phones to document anything of concern, and a list of agreed items.
3. What to check:
   - Repairs from the inspection are actually completed ({{repair_list}}).
   - Systems work — water, heat, appliances, in.
   - No new damage since the last visit.
   - Personal property and agreed inclusions are present ({{inclusions}}).
4. What to do if something is off: don't sign anything — flag it to me immediately.
5. Reassurance: I'll be there with you, and we'll handle anything that comes up calmly.

Keep it practical and reassuring — under 220 words.
```

**How to use:** Send this 2–3 days before the walkthrough. A prepared buyer is a calm buyer, and the walkthrough is where last-minute surprises either surface or fizzle.
**What good looks like:** The buyer arrives with the right checklist, knows what to look for, and flags anything unusual immediately — so issues are caught before closing, not after.

### Prompt 37: Closing gift note and review request

```
The sale of {{property_address}} just closed for {{buyer_name}} / {{seller_name}}. Write a closing-gift note and (separately) a review request message.

- Warm and personal, referencing their specific transaction.
- Explain the gift briefly ({{gift_description}}) and why it's practical for their situation.
- End with an open door and your contact block.

Review request:
- Send a few days after the gift, not in the same message.
- A genuine ask framed around helping others: "If our experience worked for you, a short review helps other buyers and sellers know what to expect."
```

**What good looks like:** The client is genuinely touched by the gift, and the review request — separated and pressure-free — converts a happy client into public proof of your work, with an honest escape hatch if anything was off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How do I personalize without spending hours on it?**
A: Personalize the two places that matter most — the opening line and the question you ask. Fill in the lead's name, the exact property or area they asked about, and one true detail from their search. The AI does the rest. Budget two minutes a lead, never ten. A fast reply that names their actual interest beats a perfect email that arrives tomorrow.

**Q: What do I do if the AI gets a property detail wrong?**
A: Fix it before it sends, and cut anything you can't verify. The pack's compliance section and the pre-publish checklist exist exactly for this. Every factual claim — square footage, beds, roof, renovations, price — must trace to the listing sheet, the MLS record, or the seller disclosure. If you can't confirm a detail, delete it. Never send an unverified feature claim to a client.

**Q: How many emails before it feels spammy?**
A: For leads: stop after three touches if there's no reply, and make the third touch the graceful exit — "I'll stop reaching out unless I hear from you." For past clients, one thoughtful touch a month is plenty, and the annual check-in is the anchor. The rule is: value on every touch, and a clean stop when someone goes silent. A well-timed silent exit protects a relationship a nagging drip would burn.

**Q: Should I disclose that I use AI?**
A: Check your jurisdiction's and brokerage's rules first — some now require disclosure. Where it's not required, be transparent anyway as a best practice. You don't need a lengthy disclaimer, but don't pass off AI-drafted copy as your own hand-written work. The ethical line is simple: AI drafts, a licensed agent reviews, owns, and sends. That's the model this whole pack is built on.

**Q: How do I handle "the market is down"?**
A: The single best answer is honest numbers, not optimism. Use Prompt 6 (cost of waiting) and the valuation prompts to show what the market actually did in the buyer's area, month by month. Acknowledge the concern directly, show the verified data, and reframe: a slower market rewards realistic pricing and a good agent. Never argue with the concern; walk them through the real numbers.

**Q: Do these work for rentals as well as sales?**
A: Yes, with light tweaks. Use the pricing prompts to explain rental comps instead of sale comps, and the lead-response prompts to qualify tenants by move-in date, budget, and desired area. The listing and social prompts work for rental ads nearly unchanged. For the transaction prompts, swap the language of sale milestones for lease-signing and move-in steps. The underlying job — respond fast, be useful, build trust — is identical.
